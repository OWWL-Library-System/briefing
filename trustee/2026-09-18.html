OWWL secured the account that same day and the library completed its SHIELD Act (GBL §899-bb) notification review with its attorney. The reporting delay was a confirmed critical violation of the [Systems Access and Confidentiality of Library Records Policy](https://owwl.org/system/policies/systems-access-and-confidentiality-of-library-records-policy/), and the library is covering the System's legal fees from the incident. - A second library received a formal warning for staff sharing a single Evergreen login instead of individual accounts, also a critical violation. - A System-wide password review found staff and circulation accounts using passwords that were years old, easily guessed, or already published on public data-breach lists (one had appeared in over 600,000 known data breaches). None met the 12-character minimum. Affected accounts have been reset, and libraries have been notified. **Multi-factor authentication (MFA) is rolling out.** Because passwords alone are not a reliable safeguard anymore, System IT is piloting MFA for email and Evergreen. MFA itself is not new or unusual. Most people already use it for banking, email, and other personal accounts, and libraries are behind the curve in adopting it for their own systems. Libraries will need to decide how to handle this new requirement. There will be options to use a physical security key or an authenticator app; a library that opts for hardware keys (a YubiKey or similar) should expect a per-device cost. Not yet a System requirement, but it is the direction every library should expect. This was discussed at length at the September 11, 2026 Directors Advisory Council Meeting, so your director should have more information. **The System Board adopted a new System and Member Library Cooperation Policy on September 9**, replacing the prior System and Member Library Relationship Policy, alongside an amended Systems Access and Confidentiality of Library Records Policy. The [Cooperation Policy](https://owwl.org/system/policies/system-and-member-library-cooperation-policy/) sets out what OWWL and its 42 member libraries owe each other. OWWL invests in the shared systems, technology support, and infrastructure every library depends on, and each library commits to the account, credential, and data security practices that keep that shared infrastructure secure and trustworthy for everyone else relying on it. Within that policy, a new [Cost Recovery Schedule](https://owwl.org/system/policies/system-and-member-library-cooperation-policy/cost-recovery-schedule/) sets prices for what a library owes when its own account or credential failures cost the System staff time or create legal exposure, from a missed on-site appointment to a security incident requiring corrective action, plus actual legal and corrective costs. As an example, the director email breach mentioned above cost just under $3,000 in legal fees and staff time. Costs like that moving forward will not be absorbed by the System. Evergreen, email, and network infrastructure are shared among all 42 libraries. A security incident at one library puts everyone at risk and pulls System IT, legal, and administrative time away from every other member library, resources built to serve all 42, not a cleanup fund for one. A library remains responsible for fixing the underlying issue regardless of any charge, and the System may suspend or restrict services for violations that put shared infrastructure at risk or continue after notice. **Why this belongs on this Board's agenda going forward.** Legal exposure is not hypothetical. The SHIELD Act requires reasonable safeguards, and New York's notification law can require notifying every affected resident, and in some cases the Attorney General, once private information is exposed. CPLR §4509 makes library borrower records confidential by law, and a shared login or a stale account undermines the System's ability to prove who actually took a given action. The Cost Recovery Schedule itself is new and untested; expect it back on this Board's agenda once there is a track record of how often, and to whom, it applies. Data security across the System depends on every library doing its part. --- ## E-Rate: Your Internet Bill Could Be About to Jump Hopefully that headline grabbed your attention. [E-Rate](https://www.fcc.gov/consumers/guides/universal-service-program-schools-and-libraries-e-rate) is the federal program, administered by [USAC](https://www.usac.org/e-rate/) under FCC oversight, that discounts internet and network costs for schools and libraries nationwide. Every year, OWWL files for E-Rate on behalf of member libraries, covering 90 percent of Category One costs (the broadband connection itself) and 85 percent of Category Two costs (the switches, firewalls, and Wi-Fi that distribute it inside the building). Most libraries never see this work happen. OWWL handles the entire filing, and the discount simply shows up. That is now at risk. If the FCC narrows or eliminates E-Rate, member libraries could see their internet and network costs rise by up to 90 percent, with no cushion in place. **The threat.** On June 25, 2026, the FCC opened a formal comment period asking whether E-Rate "should be narrowed or otherwise reoriented to reflect the extent to which its connectivity objectives have been achieved." School and library associations nationwide are raising alarm, and whether the FCC can narrow or eliminate a congressionally authorized program on its own is an unsettled legal question. **The deadlines** - **Comments due October 13, 2026.** - **Reply comments due November 12, 2026.** - Docket numbers WC 26-133, 13-184, 21-93, and 21-455. **How to file a comment** This impacts all 42 libraries, so everyone should comment. The steps below walk through how. 1. Draft your letter. Use the attached ALA comment template to personalize a letter with your library's own story, who you serve, how much E-Rate funding you receive, and what would be cut without it. You may submit it on your library's letterhead. 2. Submit it directly to the FCC. Go to the FCC's Electronic Comment Filing System's Standard Filing page, [fcc.gov/ecfs/filings/standard](https://www.fcc.gov/ecfs/filings/standard). Enter all four docket numbers, **26-133**, **13-184**, **21-93**, and **21-455**, typing each one and pressing Tab before entering the next, then attach your letter and submit your contact information. About ten minutes, no fee. **Note.** Anything you submit becomes part of the public record, including personal information in the body of your letter or on letterhead, so leave out anything you would not want made public. 3. Prefer to file jointly instead of on your own? NYLA, ALA, or a state E-Rate coordinator can carry a comment as part of a coalition filing. If you prefer to be part of OWWL's coordinated response, I will send out a form to do so. OWWL's contracted E-Rate consultant has already flagged this proceeding and can help draft language for either route. 4. Prefer step-by-step video instructions? [E-Rate Central's Comment Compass](https://e-ratecentral.com/compass/E-Rate_Comment_Compass_Landing_Page.html) walks through filing a comment from start to finish. [Download the ALA comment template](/assets/trustee-briefing/2026-09-16/save-our-e-rate-template.pdf) referenced above. --- ## Minimum Standards: A Governance Checklist New York's [Minimum Standards for Public and Association Libraries](https://nyslibrary.libguides.com/Handbook-Library-Trustees/laws-regulations#:~:text=All-,minimum%20standards,-must%20be%20addressed) (Commissioner's Regulation §90.2) set 14 baseline requirements that every member library must meet to remain in good standing. Trustees, not just the director, are legally responsible for ensuring their library meets them. See the State Library's [Helpful Information for Meeting Minimum Standards](https://nyslibrary.libguides.com/publiclibrarystandards) for the full list and detail on each one. **What are the non-compliance risks?** Each library confirms compliance annually by board resolution, motion, or statement in the minutes, and OWWL certifies all 42 member libraries to the Division of Library Development on that basis. Skipping that confirmation, or an unmet-standard variance request, makes a library ineligible for LLSA and LSSA aid, and because that aid is released to the System as a whole, one library's missing confirmation holds up funding for every other member library. Prolonged or repeated failure can also put a library at risk under Education Law, up to and including revocation of its charter. **The motion.** Any library that has not passed it yet this year may use this language, or something close to it. > [TRUSTEE NAME] moves that the [Library Name] Board of Trustees affirms that the library is in full compliance with the New York State Minimum Standards for Public and Association Libraries, as outlined in Commissioner's Regulation §90.2, and directs [NAME] to email the OWWL Library System Executive Director a link to the board meeting minutes once posted to the library's website, within two weeks of the meeting, in accordance with New York State Open Meetings Law. [TRUSTEE NAME] seconds, and the motion passed unanimously. A motion that does not capture the actual action taken will not satisfy this requirement. One question worth checking at the local level is whether this library's board has formally confirmed Minimum Standards compliance for the current year, and whether that confirmation appears in the minutes rather than only in the director's own records. [Download the Minimum Standards Compliance Checklist](/assets/trustee-briefing/2026-09-16/minimum-standards-compliance-checklist.docx), covering all 14 Minimum Standards with this motion and the minutes-notification step included as action items. --- ## Required Policies Due: January 1, 2027 Standard 4 of the Minimum Standards requires written operating policies, reviewed at least every five years. New York's Not-for-Profit Corporation Law §715 requires a board to identify and manage related-party transactions, where a trustee or officer stands to benefit financially from a board decision. Disclosure, recusal, and documentation in the minutes are the standard response, and most conflict-of-interest policies are built around exactly this. A larger set of situations creates tension without breaking any law or policy. - A Friends-of-the-Library officer or active volunteer who also serves as a trustee, where the Friends group's priorities are exactly what the trustee is also asked to weigh independently. - A trustee's close friend or family member joining the same board, where personal loyalty is hard to fully separate from institutional judgment on a difficult personnel matter or a contested vote. - Someone who runs a different library serving as a trustee elsewhere. Two peer libraries are not affiliates under the Not-for-Profit Corporation Law's related-party rules, so this does not trigger that framework; however, a person's professional frame of reference is still shaped by the institution they run full-time. - A vendor, contractor, or landlord who does routine business with the library and also sits on its board. - A local elected official whose other public role intersects with the library's funding or facilities. Education Law §260(4) only disqualifies a sitting member of the municipal council or board that appoints library trustees from serving as a trustee in that same municipality, and only for municipal public libraries with council-appointed boards; other overlaps fall outside that narrow rule. **Why name these even when nothing has gone wrong?** A board does not need proof of misconduct to decide that a seat, or a pattern, does not serve its own effectiveness. Building eligibility or recusal norms around patterns like these is common, well-accepted nonprofit board practice, protecting the board's decision-making from divided loyalty, not only catching money changing hands. **What a board can do about it.** Without a legal finding first, a board may want to consider a few options. - Check whether the local conflict-of-interest policy's disclosure requirements cover non-financial affiliations, not only financial ones. - Ask new trustee candidates to disclose affiliations like these at nomination. - Use recusal from a specific vote where that is enough. - Consider bylaw-based eligibility criteria where a pattern is a standing concern. --- ## Lessons Learned in Recent OSC Audits The State Comptroller audits local governments across New York every week. None of the audits below are of libraries, but the same gaps keep showing up, and every one is a preview of what an auditor, or a news story, could just as easily find at a library. ### [A village: Records and Reports](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/village/2026/06/12/village-whitehall-records-and-reports-2026m-9) A board that stops doing its own required annual audit of the treasurer's or director's records is how errors compound for years unnoticed. One village's clerk-treasurer kept incomplete records and skipped required monthly reports, and the board never conducted its own required annual audit. Fund balance was later found overstated by more than $870,000. **Takeaways for libraries** - Put the full board's required annual audit of the treasurer's or director's records on the calendar every year, and document it in the minutes. - Ask whether monthly, not just annual, financial reports are reaching the board, and whether anyone reviews them closely enough to catch an error early. ### [A town: Transparency of Fiscal Activities](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/statewide-audit/2026/06/12/town-lewis-transparency-fiscal-activities-s9-25-31) Delegating the annual audit informally to one or two board members instead of the full board creates the same blind spot. One town's board delegated its financial audit to two of its own members, kept no documentation of it, and went three consecutive years without filing its Annual Financial Report. **Takeaways for libraries** - The annual audit belongs to the full board, not a subset of members acting informally. - Filing required financial reports on schedule is a compliance requirement with real consequences, including loss of registration and funding eligibility. ### [A third town: Host Fee Payments](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/town/2026/06/18/town-schuyler-falls-host-fee-payments-2026m-26) Recurring revenue or payments need a verification step, not just a receipt. A landfill operator's miscalculated host fee payments went uncaught for nearly a decade, costing one town over $460,000 across 35 of 39 payments reviewed, because no one checked the math. **Takeaways for libraries** - Give any recurring payment or revenue source your library receives a documented verification step. Receiving a check is not the same as confirming the amount is correct. ### Building Access: Two Audits, One Pattern Access reviews fail when no one owns the job, and the gap runs in both directions. One BOCES had 1,535 active badges, with 102 employees holding two or more, because duplicate and outdated credentials were never cleaned up. A separate school district deactivated 177 badges issued to local first responders during an access cleanup, and they stayed off until auditors caught it. **Takeaways for libraries** - Name an owner for reviewing building access on a recurring basis, for employees and non-employees alike. - A library's own access points, including after-hours trustee access and evening programs, deserve the same periodic review. Ask who has a key or a code, and confirm someone actually checks. **Audits in this batch:** - [A BOCES](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/school-district/2026/07/31/orange-ulster-board-cooperative-educational-services-building-access-s9) - [A school district](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/school-district/2026/07/31/new-york-mills-union-free-school-district-building-access-s9-26-18) ### Cybersecurity: Pay Close Attention to This One Cybersecurity is now a standing audit focus for local governments of every size. This is the section to read closely alongside the [data security work already underway across the System](#data-security-multi-factor-authentication-and-cost-recovery-what-the-board-adopted). Recent audits at a town and two villages examined cybersecurity practices directly and found the same kind of gap driving this System's own recent incidents, informal practice standing in for a written policy. **Takeaways for libraries** - If your library has never had a cybersecurity-specific review, separate from its general financial audit, this is a good year to ask for one. - Cybersecurity scrutiny is reaching entities of every size. Libraries should expect the same eventually. **Audits in this batch:** - [A town](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/town/2026/08/14/town-wilton-cybersecurity-2026m-48) - [A village](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/village/2026/08/07/village-cassadaga-cybersecurity-2026m-25) - [A second village](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/village/2026/08/07/village-lacona-cybersecurity-2026m-23) > Across all of these audits, a consistent pattern appears. Problems rarely begin with fraud or misconduct. They begin with small gaps in oversight, unclear documentation, or policies that drift out of alignment with actual practice. Regular review of financial procedures, procurement policies, and board reporting practices helps prevent those small gaps from becoming larger governance failures. --- ## Thank You to the Boards That Approved the Direct Access Plan Commissioner's Regulation §90.3 requires a majority of member library boards to approve the 2027 to 2031 Direct Access Plan before OWWL Library System's own Plan of Service can be submitted to the New York State Education Department.
