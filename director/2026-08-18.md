## Phishing: Increased Activity, and a Recent Example

OWWL's Computer and Network Services (CANS) department sent the following notice to all staff:

Over the past couple of weeks, we've seen an increase in phishing emails targeting staff across the OWWL System. Many of these phishing messages threaten that your email account or password needs to be validated or confirmed, or your account will be suspended or deactivated.

While similar (and even identical) phishing emails have been seen before, the timing of this increase is concerning, as some of you may have recently received legitimate mandatory password reset notifications for email and/or Evergreen. We want to make sure everyone knows how to recognize these phishing attempts and what to do if you receive one. **Please read this email carefully.**

Please know that OWWL will **never**:

- Ask you to review, validate, confirm, upgrade, or update your account;
- Ask you to "authenticate" your information;
- Ask you to confirm your current password in order to keep using it;
- Hold or withhold your emails from being delivered for not acting on any of the above. The only time you may not receive emails is if your mailbox is full. Depending on which version of Zimbra you use, you can check your mailbox storage by:
  - Classic Client: Hovering your mouse over the bar underneath your name in the top right corner.
  - Modern Client: Clicking the gear icon in the top right corner, then clicking Settings.

If you receive an email asking you to do any of the above, **it is phishing**. All of these tactics aim to get your email username and password so the cybercriminals sending these messages can take over your account. **Do not click any links, do not download any attachments, do not provide your account usernames or passwords, and do not reply.**

A few tips to protect yourself:

- Always check the sender's actual email address, not just the display name. Cybercriminals often spoof a familiar name (like "owwl Support" or "Owwl Admin Team") while the underlying address is unrelated (like "info@advanceservs.com"). Any legitimate email from OWWL staff or an Evergreen password reset will come from an @owwl.org address. Even then, some cybercriminals can spoof the email address, so it's important to look out for other red flags.
- Be suspicious of urgency or threats, such as messages claiming your account will be suspended, deactivated, or locked if you don't act immediately or within a narrow timeframe (24-48 hours is a common threat). Legitimate requests from OWWL will never pressure you this way.
- When in doubt, don't click. Forward the email to `support` to confirm whether a message is legitimate. **Do not forward suspected phishing emails to individual OWWL staff.** It is important that our entire team sees these emails so we can send timely alerts if needed.
- Instead of just deleting a phishing email, mark it as spam/junk. This teaches Zimbra to block the sender so additional emails from that address are stopped before they reach anyone else's inbox. It takes multiple spam reports to block a sender, so the more people who report these messages as spam, the more effective it is.

If you've received a suspicious email and believe you may have already clicked a link or entered your username/password somewhere you shouldn't have, please open a support ticket right away so we can help.

If you are interested in learning more about phishing and seeing some common examples, [check out this page on OWWL Docs](https://docs.owwl.org/Members/CommonEmailPhishingScam).

Thank you for staying alert and helping keep our System secure.

## Password Security: A Good Excuse to Check Your Own Setup

This week's WNYLIB-L Tech-Talk covered email passwords, and it's worth passing along the plain version of the advice.

Your email password matters more than almost any other one you have. Most other accounts, banking, shopping, benefits portals, can be reset with a link sent straight to your inbox. If someone gets into your email, they can use that same reset process to work their way into everything else.

None of this requires a complicated setup. You don't need a password manager if you don't want one. The two things that actually matter are: use a long, unique password for your email account (12 characters or more is a good target, and a memorable passphrase works fine), and don't reuse that password anywhere else. If you'd rather write it down than memorize it, that's fine too, as long as it's kept somewhere secure, a locked drawer, not a sticky note on the monitor.

## A Recurring Reminder: Don't Enter Driver's License Numbers in Evergreen

A weekly internal report flagged driver's license numbers entered into new patron registrations in Evergreen again this month. Please pass this along to library staff: driver's license numbers **must never** be typed into Evergreen, for any patron, for any reason. Any numbers found have already been removed from the affected records.

**Why this matters beyond policy**

New York's data breach notification law (General Business Law §899-aa, as amended by the SHIELD Act) defines "private information" to include a driver's license number combined with a person's name, and requires notifying every affected New York resident, and in some cases the Attorney General and other state offices, if that combination is exposed in a breach. Evergreen is deliberately built not to hold that combination for your protection. Registration is designed to collect only what circulation requires, and OWWL runs an automated sweep that scans new records for identification-number patterns and strips them out. That sweep catches patterns, though, not intent, and it isn't a substitute for the number never being entered in the first place. A driver's license number that sits in a record for even a short window before that sweep runs is a driver's license number that could be exposed if a breach happens to land in that window, and once it's exposed, the notification obligation, and the letters, the possible Attorney General involvement, and the legal and reputational cost that comes with it, attaches to something an ordinary patron registration otherwise wouldn't carry at all.

**What to tell your staff**

- Never enter a driver's license number into any field in Evergreen, including notes fields, even when a patron offers it as ID.
- A driver's license is fine to look at for identity verification at the desk. It just shouldn't be typed into the system anywhere.
- If a number was entered by mistake, remove it. If it cannot be found, open a support ticket right away so it can be removed immediately rather than waiting for the automated sweep to catch it.

This isn't a criticism of any particular staff member. Registration moves fast, and a driver's license is a natural thing to reach for when verifying identity. The point is just to keep that number out of the system entirely, since removing it after the fact only closes part of the exposure window.

## Gift Cards, Prizes, and Public Funds: What the Law Actually Says

A gift-card question came up recently at Directors' Q&A that we did not have time to dive into. Here is an article to help libraries think through this situation. 

**Question:** Can a library buy gift cards to use as summer reading prizes? 

**Answer:** The honest answer is that this isn't fully settled, and it changes depending on where the money comes from and what kind of library is asking. Here's what the actual sources say.

### The starting point: the gift of public funds clause

Article VIII, Section 1 of the NY Constitution prohibits counties, cities, towns, villages, and school districts from giving public money "to or in aid of any individual, or private corporation or association" without getting something of value back in return. The Comptroller's office has described the purpose of that clause as preventing "raids on the public purse for the benefit of favored individuals or enterprises furnishing no corresponding public benefit or consideration."

Worth noting: that list of covered entities doesn't include "library" or "library district" by name. Whether it reaches a particular library's own spending depends on how that library is chartered.

- A **school district public library** is literally on the list.
- A **municipal public library** is close to a municipality, and Comptroller audit practice treats its tax-levy spending the same way it would treat the municipality's own spending, though that's institutional practice rather than the constitutional text naming the library directly.
- An **association library** is a private not-for-profit education corporation, not a municipal corporation or school district. The constitutional clause, on its face, doesn't appear to reach it directly. A different framework, the fiduciary duty not to use charitable assets outside the library's mission, is the more likely real constraint there, and that's a different legal question with different rules.

### What's actually documented about gifts to individuals

The clearest, most directly on-point guidance isn't about libraries at all. It's about municipal and school district employee recognition. The Comptroller's Opinion 99-11 confirms that municipalities and school districts can give employees years-of-service pins, plaques, or similar mementos "of nominal value" as part of a recognition program, on the reasoning that the personal benefit is incidental to a legitimate public purpose. What consistently shows up across that line of guidance is nominal value, non-cash, and tied to a recognition purpose. Cash and cash equivalents don't show up as an approved category anywhere in that guidance.

The State Education Department's own Grants Finance office uses similar language in a different context. Its guidance on federal and state grant spending states plainly that "the State Comptroller has determined that 'favors' represent gifts of public funds which are unallowable under the State Constitution," in a discussion of why even something like a pen or notepad shouldn't be purchased with grant money and given away. That guidance is written for grant administration specifically, not library operating budgets, but it invokes the constitutional theory directly, not a grant-only rule.

### The most library-specific data point

The State Library's own "Love Your Library" Special Revenue Fund, a fund that exists to support library programs, lists its ineligible uses directly, and "refreshments, giveaways, prizes, awards" is on that list. That's not a ruling that no library funds anywhere can ever pay for a prize. It's one fund's own eligibility rule. But it's the clearest example of the state agency that oversees libraries drawing this exact line, in a document written specifically about library programs.

### The other side of it

None of this means the answer is a flat no. Public funds spent for a genuine public purpose aren't automatically a "gift" just because a private individual benefits from them, as long as that benefit is incidental and the public gets something real in return. That's the same reasoning that lets a library spend tax-levy or donated funds distributing food and PPE to its community, provided the board ties it to the library's mission by resolution first. A summer reading incentive tied explicitly to a library's literacy mission, adopted the same way, is a reasonable candidate for that same argument. There's no Comptroller opinion or audit finding, that could be located, either applying or rejecting that reasoning for a prize or incentive specifically. It's an open question, not a closed one.

### Where the caution consistently lands anyway

Two things line up across every source here, regardless of where you land on the constitutional question. First, gift cards are cash equivalents, and every piece of guidance that distinguishes cash from non-cash items treats cash more strictly. A book, a toy, or a t-shirt purchased for a program isn't the same category as a gift card, even if they cost the same. Second, funding a prize through donated gift cards or the Friends group sidesteps the constitutional question almost entirely, since that's private money, not public money, and existing guidance treats a donated or sponsor-provided gift card as fine under an ordinary donation-acceptance policy.

There's no single clean rule to hand you here. What exists is a constitutional principle that clearly covers cash gifts to individuals in other contexts, a state library funding guideline that excludes prizes specifically, and an open, untested argument on the other side for programs tied closely to a library's core mission. Where your library lands on that spectrum, and whether it's worth testing the untested argument with library funds versus routing it through the Friends group instead, is worth a conversation with your own board and, if you want it in writing, your library's own counsel.

**Sources:** NY Constitution, Article VIII, §1; [OSC Opinion 91-32](https://www.osc.ny.gov/legal-opinions/opinion-91-32); [OSC Opinion 99-11](https://www.osc.ny.gov/legal-opinions/opinion-99-11); NYSED Grants Finance FAQ, "Can small 'favors' (e.g., pens, notepads) be purchased and given away under federal and State grants?"; [NYSL Love Your Library Special Revenue Fund guidelines](https://www.nysl.nysed.gov/libdev/lylplate/guidelines).

## Member Libraries and Agreements: What OWWL Library System Can and Can't Be Committed To

There has been a recurring issue of libraries misrepresenting their organizations by claiming they're speaking on behalf of OWWL Library System. A member library can enter into its own contracts, ask its own vendors for quotes, and set up its own partnerships within the context of what their own board has allowed. What a member library cannot do is enter an agreement, request pricing, or describe a partnership on behalf of OWWL Library System or on behalf of any other member library.

This isn't a new rule. The System and Member Library Relationship Policy states directly that "the OWWL Library System and its member libraries operate as separate entities," and that "member libraries and external organizations may not use OWWL Library System information for business purposes (e.g., vendor negotiations, pricing, contracts) without explicit written approval." Each member library is independently governed by its own Board of Trustees, and OWWL Library System is its own legal entity with its own board. Neither one can bind the other to an agreement without that entity's own authorization. The [System and Member Library Relationship Policy](https://owwl.org/system/policies/system-and-member-library-relationship-policy/) has the full language.

This goes beyond OWWL's own policy, too. Representing that you have authority to bind another organization when you don't isn't just a policy violation. Under general principles of New York agency and misrepresentation law, someone who falsely claims authority to act for another party can be held personally liable to anyone who reasonably relied on that claim, independent of anything OWWL's own policy says.

The [Name, Branding, and Identity Use Policy](https://owwl.org/system/policies/name-branding-identity-use-policy/) adds a related piece. Any description of a relationship with OWWL Library System, whether by name or by referencing the counties it serves collectively, needs approval from this office before it's printed or distributed. That's true even when the underlying partnership itself is a good one.

Recently, a member library had been discussing a proposed sponsorship with a local business for several months. By the time this office learned of it, a draft agreement described the arrangement as a partnership between the business and OWWL Library System itself, not the individual library. No such partnership existed. OWWL Library System is a legal entity separate from any individual member library, and the member library's own conversations with the business didn't create an agreement on the System's behalf.

None of this is a knock on the partnerships themselves. Shared programs and vendor relationships that reach across the System are exactly the kind of thing worth pursuing. The fix is sequencing. Contact this office before anything gets printed, distributed, or described publicly as involving OWWL Library System or the counties it serves together. As a cooperative system (a different legal structure than a federated system), OWWL Library System needs a written agreement, contract, or MOU in place, and needs to sign off on any relationship with an outside vendor or partner, before that relationship is described publicly.

If a partnership idea involves anything beyond your own library, reach out early. It's much easier to build the right agreement from the start than to unwind printed materials after the fact.

## This Week in OSC Audits

Seven of this week's Office of the State Comptroller audits looked at building access controls, six at school districts and one at a BOCES, and six of them found the same underlying weakness. Fire district and fire company audits are excluded from this summary, per standing practice.

### [School District — Building Access](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/school-district/2026/07/31/holley-central-school-district-building-access-s9-26-4)

Of 423 active building-access accounts, 14 employees held multiple key fobs (one, a Working Supervisor, had four), and 27 non-employee fobs, including 18 issued to former contractors, stayed active with no one ever reviewing whether they were still needed.

### [School District — Building Access](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/school-district/2026/07/31/red-hook-central-school-district-building-access-s9-26-7)

A larger district's 827 active accounts told the same story: 37 employees and 19 non-employees held duplicate badges, and 28 non-employee badges stayed active after they were no longer needed. The board's own badge-issuance policy didn't match how badges were actually being issued or reviewed.

### [School District — Building Access](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/school-district/2026/07/31/batavia-city-school-district-building-access-s9-26-10)

Sixteen employees held 34 unneeded key fobs between them (one employee had four active at once), and seven non-employee fobs plus four unaccounted-for shared fobs, with no record of who they'd been issued to, remained active. Auditors traced it to the same root cause as the others: no periodic review of whether an account was still needed.

### [School District — Building Access](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/school-district/2026/07/31/central-square-central-school-district-building-access-s9-26-14)

Of 947 active accounts, nine employees held duplicate badges and 19 non-employee badges, including 11 issued to former employees, remained active. A periodic review process existed on paper here, but it didn't catch any of this.

### [School District — Building Access](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/school-district/2026/07/31/north-collins-central-school-district-building-access-s9-26-16)

Of 280 active accounts, 17 non-employee accounts and 6 shared badges stayed active well past their need. The district had a process for adding accounts but none for removing them: "no one periodically reviewed active accounts to determine whether they were needed."

### [A BOCES — Building Access](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/school-district/2026/07/31/orange-ulster-board-cooperative-educational-services-building-access-s9)

The largest system of the week, 1,535 active accounts: 102 employees held two or more active badges (six had three), 30 non-employee badges went untracked, and 28 badges couldn't be located at all, including 16 duplicates. Written procedures requiring regular badge reviews existed. They just weren't followed.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- Whoever issues a building-access badge or key fob should also own disabling it. If no one is responsible for that second step, badges outlive the person who needed them.
- A periodic access review, comparing active badges against actual current staff and contractors, is what catches this before an auditor does. None of the districts above had one that actually worked.
- A written policy that doesn't match actual practice is as good as no policy, as one of the districts above found out. If your board has adopted a badge or key-issuance policy, check that it still describes what's actually happening.

### [School District — Building Access](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/school-district/2026/07/31/new-york-mills-union-free-school-district-building-access-s9-26-18)

This one had the opposite problem. The district's account management was otherwise solid, clear responsibilities, written procedures, real reviews, but that same review turned up 177 badges issued to local first responders that had been deactivated and never reactivated. Auditors flagged it, and the district turned them back on immediately, but for a time, police and fire personnel could have found themselves locked out of school buildings in an emergency.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- Access control cuts both ways. A review process built only to catch badges that should be off can just as easily miss ones that were wrongly turned off. If your library grants after-hours access to a fire company, local police, or a similar partner, make sure a deactivation done for one purpose doesn't quietly take out access someone else still needs.

> Across all of these audits, a consistent pattern appears. Problems rarely begin with fraud or misconduct. They begin with small gaps in oversight, unclear documentation, or policies that drift out of alignment with actual practice. Regular review of financial procedures, procurement policies, and board reporting practices helps prevent those small gaps from becoming larger governance failures.
