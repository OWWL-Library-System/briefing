## E-Rate: Help Build OWWL's Comment to the FCC

*Rerun from Yesterday's Email*

The FCC's review of the E-Rate program is no longer just something to watch. It now has deadlines, and I am asking every library to weigh in.

**What is new since the last update.** The FCC's notice (FCC 26-41) was published in the Federal Register on August 14. Public comments are due October 13, 2026.

The core issue is whether E-Rate, which covers roughly 90 percent of your library's internet bill (Category 1) and 80 percent of your wifi and network equipment costs (Category 2), should continue in its current form, be scaled back, or end.

If the program ends, library internet bills will go up significantly, an increase of $1,600 to $7,100 a year depending on your library, and it affects every library in our System. It also means that wifi access points, switches, and any other network equipment currently provided through E-Rate would need to be paid for by your library instead.

I cannot stress the critical nature of this situation enough.

**What I am asking every library to do, by September 30.**

1. Fill out this short [E-Rate input form](https://forms.gle/U9x7Ceenp9nD6pXq7) describing how E-Rate funding supports your library and what is at stake if it is cut. I will use these responses to build a System-wide comment representing all 42 OWWL libraries. *(Thirteen of you have already filled out the form since yesterday — nice job!)*
2. Tell me if you would also like to file your own individual comment. E-Rate Central's free [Comment Compass for Libraries](https://e-ratecentral.com/compass/E-Rate_Comment_Compass_for_Libraries.html) makes this a 10 to 15 minute task, and I am happy to help.
3. Tell your board about the significance of this issue, so members understand that costs will increase without this program.
4. Let me know, through the form, if you or your trustees have questions about the E-Rate program, how it affects libraries, or how it works with our System.
5. Share [ALA's E-Rate take action campaign](https://www.ala.org/advocacy/federal-resources/broadband-policy/erate) with your Trustees, so they have a direct way to weigh in with their own elected officials.

The System has filed E-Rate on behalf of member libraries for years as an in-kind service; now I need your help in return. My goal is full participation, either a comment or input, from all 42 member libraries by September 30, so we can submit our System-wide comment well ahead of the FCC's October 13 filing date. All comments are public.

This is a straightforward way to put your library's voice on the federal record about funding that directly affects your budget.

## Electronic Recycling Event: Drop-Off Dates and What We Accept

OWWL is holding an electronic recycling drop-off again this year, running Tuesdays and Thursdays, 10:00 AM to 3:00 PM, from October 1 through November 24.

The drop-off accepts old library computers, monitors, printers, network equipment (routers, firewalls, modems), and accessories such as mice, keyboards, and cables. The full accepted-materials list from our recycling partner is broader than that. It also covers servers and other data center equipment, office equipment such as copiers and phone systems, mobile devices, and small household electronics such as power tools and fitness watches. It does not accept devices containing mercury, refrigerant-bearing appliances, loose or damaged batteries, CDs and DVDs, or large appliances.

Please do not send electronic recycling through delivery. When you arrive, park behind the building next to the garage door and notify CANS staff that you are there. A sign posted on the garage door has a phone number to call.

Some computers and monitors brought in for recycling may instead be kept and reused for System-wide programs. If you would rather your equipment go directly to e-waste recycling, let CANS know by email or at drop-off.

If the scheduled days and times do not work for your library, or if you need help transporting equipment, contact `support`.

For the full list of accepted materials, refer to Matthew's email to directors.

## Library Card Sign-Up Month Highlight: Dansville Public Library

Great work to Margo and the team at Dansville Public Library on their Library Card Sign-Up Month video.

The video is a good example of promoting the library without putting any patron or staff data at risk. Evergreen appears on screen, but only on the main landing page, and a dummy barcode is scanned into the catalog search bar rather than a patron record. No patron or staff information is visible anywhere in the shot. The library also kept the video grounded in familiar faces, the staff patrons actually see and interact with every day.

It is worth being just as intentional when your own library promotes Library Card Sign-Up Month. Scanning an OWWL library card for a photo might seem harmless, but it violates policy and rarely looks good on screen either.

Again, well done, Margo!

## Public Computer Black Screens: Fixed, With a Reminder on Patron Privacy

OWWL's Computer and Network Services (CANS) department sent the following notice:

Hi everyone,

The CANS team has received some reports of public computers starting up and only showing a black desktop. [This was caused by an issue with a recent Windows security patch.](https://www.forbes.com/sites/barrycollins/2026/09/04/botched-windows-11-update-turns-desktop-black/) We are currently rolling out a temporary fix.

In the meantime, public computers will likely retain patron activity that is normally cleared after a restart. Before they are done with their computer session, patrons should check the desktop as well as the Documents and Downloads folders. If they created and saved or downloaded any files during their session, the patron should delete them so they go to the Recycling Bin, then empty the Recycling Bin to permanently delete the files.

Web browsers should still function as normal--when closing the browser, it should log patrons out of accounts and delete the browsing history, but it doesn't hurt for patrons to double check that they are logged out of important accounts.

The fix should be applied to all libraries no later than this afternoon. If you still are encountering public computers with black screens after that, please open a support ticket to let us know.

Thanks for your patience.

## Staff Conduct: Patron Relationships and Outside Ties

Most library conduct policies cover the obvious ground. Attendance, dress code, workplace behavior, confidentiality of patron records. Two areas get less attention, and both come up more often than you would expect. Both of the situations below reflect recent situations across OWWL member libraries and each comes with something a director can do about it.

### Personal relationships with patrons

A staff member dating, or otherwise personally involved with, a regular patron is not something most library conduct policies address directly, and it is not something a library has grounds to discipline someone for on its own. An off-duty relationship between two adults is their own business.

What is within a library's authority is conduct on the clock. A few examples worth watching for.

- Extra time, attention, or preferential service at the desk.
- Personal conversations that pull focus from other patrons or duties.
- Phone calls, texting, or an emotional conversation with coworkers happening on the clock.

None of these are really about the relationship. They are ordinary performance and conduct issues, the same as any other on-duty personal matter would be.

**What this means for your library:** A short addition to a personnel policy or handbook, framed around on-duty conduct rather than who staff can or cannot date, gives a director something concrete to point to if a similar situation comes up. It does not need to name relationships specifically. General language about maintaining professional boundaries and full attention to duties during working hours covers the same ground without wading into anyone's personal life.

### Outside affiliations and balanced service

A staff member with a connection to an outside organization (a nonprofit board seat, a volunteer role, a family business) is not automatically barred from acting on library matters that touch that organization. It is still worth a second look before they do.

A library exists to serve its whole community, not any one group within it. When a staff member with a tie to an outside organization is the one making a service decision (a program partnership, a meeting room booking, a collection request), that decision risks getting read as favoritism, fairly or not, even when the staff member's judgment is sound. How the decision looks matters here almost as much as whether it was actually fair.

**What this means for your library:** This does not require a formal outside-affiliations policy with disclosure forms and a review committee. Most libraries do not need that level of structure. It is often enough for a director to know what ties exist on staff, and to make the call themselves, or hand it to someone without that same tie, when a request touches an organization a staff member is personally involved with.

### What this supports

Neither situation assumes bad faith. Most staff in both cases are acting in good faith the whole time. A library's obligation to serve everyone evenhandedly holds up better when personal relationships and outside ties stay separate from on-the-job decisions, and a little forethought here heads off a harder conversation later.

## This Week in OSC Audits

### Building Access: Three School Districts, One Pattern

Three school district audits this cycle looked at building access controls, and each one found the same underlying problem: nobody was regularly reviewing who still needed an active key card or fob, so accounts piled up long after they should have been closed out. One district's maintenance supervisor held three active fobs he did not need, and eight non-employee fobs remained active with no ongoing purpose; two shared fobs could not be located at all. A second district could not locate seventeen non-employees' duplicate active key cards, nine of which had never even been used, and eighteen more cards remained active despite showing no activity history. The most severe of the three managed 1,170 active accounts total; 106 people held duplicate badges, 56 non-employee badges were no longer needed, and staff could not locate 37 of the 78 badges auditors asked to see, nearly half.

None of the three districts had a written policy assigning anyone responsibility for periodically reviewing who still needed access.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- Check whether your written badge or key policy matches what staff actually do day to day, not just what is on paper.
- Name one staff member responsible for reviewing the full list of active badges, fobs, or keys at least once a year, employees and non-employees alike.
- Set a firm point at which a non-employee's access (a volunteer, a contractor, a former board member) gets turned off, and confirm someone actually does it.
- Keep a physical inventory of every issued key, fob, or badge, and periodically confirm you can actually locate them, not just that a system shows them as active.

### Financial Management: Two Different Ways to Get Fund Balance Wrong

Two financial management audits this cycle showed opposite problems with the same root cause: nobody was giving the board a clear, accurate picture of the government's finances. A city's unrestricted fund balance fell roughly 16 percent over five years and stayed below its own policy threshold for four straight years, and the council could not agree on how to fix it, partly because the policy itself had no procedure for getting back on track. The audit also found the city auditor using improper accounting practices, netting revenue against expenses and posting transactions to the wrong accounts, which left officials without reliable numbers to work from. Annual Financial Reports were filed late for three straight years, and later reports contained inaccuracies auditors called deliberate.

A school district went the other direction. It accumulated more fund balance than it reasonably needed, used improper year-end reserve transfers to stay just under the statutory 4 percent surplus cap, and held two reserves totaling $977,890 with no documented justification for the amount. The board's own reserve reports were incomplete, which left members unable to evaluate what was actually being held and why.

Neither government had an up-to-date multiyear financial plan to guide these decisions.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- Adopt a written fund balance policy with a target range, and make sure it also spells out what happens if you fall outside that range.
- Give your board full, itemized reserve reports rather than summary totals, so members can actually evaluate what is being held and why.
- Build and keep a multiyear financial plan current rather than budgeting one year at a time.
- File Annual Financial Reports on time, with numbers your board would recognize as accurate.

**Audits in this batch:**

- [A city](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/city/2026/09/04/city-olean-financial-management-2026m-40)
- [A fourth school district](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/school-district/2026/09/11/honeoye-central-school-district-financial-management-2026m-67)

### [A fifth school district: Financial Condition](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/school-district/2026/08/28/fulton-city-school-district-financial-condition-2026m-71)

This district's surplus fund balance dropped to $607,034, about 0.6 percent of the next year's budget, which leaves almost no cushion for an emergency. The board balanced two straight budgets by appropriating fund balance while underestimating expenses by roughly $4 million combined, producing deficits of about $5.3 million. The cash flow strain got serious enough that the district issued $8 million in revenue anticipation notes to cover year-end obligations, and officials had not reviewed or updated their multiyear financial plans.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- Do not rely on drawing down fund balance to balance the budget year after year. Eventually there is nothing left to draw down.
- Keep multiyear financial plans current, not just the current year's budget.
- Watch cash flow throughout the year rather than waiting until a shortfall forces a short-term borrowing.

### [A village: Budgeting and Financial Management](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/village/2026/08/28/village-south-glens-falls-budgeting-and-financial-management-2026m-49)

The village board routinely overestimated its appropriations and did not regularly compare budget to actual results during the year. Over time this built up $3.5 million in reserves that were never governed by an adopted fund balance or reserve policy, a sign that taxes and fees were set higher than they needed to be. Auditors noted that similar concerns were raised in a 2014 audit and a follow-up review, and the village did not take sufficient corrective action either time.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- Adopt written fund balance and reserve policies if you do not already have them, and revisit them on a set schedule rather than only when an auditor asks.
- Compare budget to actual results during the year, not only after it closes.
- Treat a prior audit's findings as a checklist for the next one, not a closed chapter.

### Supervisor's Records and Reports: Two Towns, Books Nobody Was Watching

Two towns handed their bookkeeping to an outside firm and stopped watching what it did. In one, the bookkeeping firm made bank transfers, processed payroll, and posted journal entries without the Supervisor's approval, and $23,034 in unauthorized payments to the Highway Superintendent went undetected as a result. The Supervisor also destroyed bank statements and kept records on a cash basis rather than the required modified accrual basis, so officials could not properly monitor the town's financial condition, and Annual Financial Reports for four straight years were not filed until 2025.

The second town's problem was worse. Over two years, $4.9 million in revenue and $4 million in disbursements were never entered into the accounting system, because only January's transactions were recorded each year. The monthly reports the board did see overstated receipts by $14,095 and disbursements by $212,431, and nobody prepared bank reconciliations for the Supervisor to review. The 2023 Annual Financial Report was filed 580 days late, and the 2024 and 2025 reports still had not been filed as of April 2026.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- If you delegate bookkeeping to an outside firm, keep reviewing and approving what it does. Delegating the task is not the same as delegating the oversight.
- Reconcile bank statements every month, and have someone other than the bookkeeper review them.
- Enter revenue and disbursements as they happen, not once a year.
- File your Annual Financial Report on time. It is one of the few outside checks that runs between full audits.

**Audits in this batch:**

- [A town](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/town/2026/08/21/town-portage-supervisors-records-and-reports-2026m-61)
- [A second town](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/town/2026/08/21/town-rose-supervisors-records-and-reports-2026m-63)

### [A third town: Salaries and Employee Benefits](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/town/2026/08/28/town-galway-salaries-and-employee-benefits-2026m-19)

Auditors found $27,215 in payroll and benefits errors over roughly three and a half years. Eight salaried employees were paid $15,486 without documented board approval, while two others were underpaid $922. Three employees contributed $9,174 less than required toward health insurance premiums because the policy was not applied consistently. A highway employee exceeded the contractual sick leave limit, and four of five part-time employees used 40 hours of leave they were not entitled to. The audit put it plainly: the Supervisor did not adequately monitor salaries and benefits against what the board had actually approved.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- Check payroll and benefit deductions against board-approved resolutions on a regular schedule, not just when someone is hired.
- Apply health insurance contribution rates the same way for every employee in the same category.
- Track leave accrual and use against contractual limits rather than relying on staff to self-report.

### [A sixth school district: Credit Cards](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/school-district/2026/09/11/south-huntington-union-free-school-credit-cards-2025m-41)

The claims auditor did not review 13 of 32 credit card claims, totaling $73,628, before they were paid, including $57,232 in fuel card claims. Of 189 general purchases reviewed, 31 had no itemized receipt and 17 had no receipt at all. Auditors also found $7,401 in purchases by people who were not authorized to use a card, $7,040 in purchases for meals and other discretionary items, $5,378 in purchases unrelated to district operations, and $9,138 in purchases that violated the district's own purchasing policy, including expenses from a board retreat. On the fuel card side, the district had no written policy, incomplete inventory records, no fuel log in four departments, and $12,054 in unsupported transactions out of $165,293 spent.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- Require an itemized receipt for every credit card purchase, with no exceptions.
- Have your claims auditor actually review every credit card claim before it is paid, not a sample of them.
- Put a written fuel card policy in place if your library has vehicles, and keep a fuel log for every department that uses one.
- Keep a current list of who is authorized to use a card, and check purchases against it.

### [A second village: Capital Project](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/village/2026/09/11/village-groton-capital-project-2026m-36)

The village competitively procured the original $3.7 million construction contracts for a $4.1 million municipal building and fire station project, but did not do the same for $135,900 in later purchases tied to the project. Of that, $99,100 violated General Municipal Law and $36,800 did not comply with the village's own procurement policy. As the audit put it, the board cannot be sure the village got quality goods and services at the lowest possible cost.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- Competitive bidding rules apply to every purchase tied to a capital project, not only the original contract.
- Check every project-related purchase against both state procurement law and your own procurement policy before it goes out, not after.

### [A seventh school district: Investment Program](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/school-district/2026/09/11/beekmantown-central-school-district-investment-program-2026m-62)

The district's investments were legal, safe, and liquid, but officials never built an actual investment strategy around them. With an average of $15.9 million available each month across 14 accounts, the district earned only $34,046 in investment income. Auditors calculated that moving those funds into higher-yield accounts the district already had access to could have earned more than $1 million more. Officials had not prepared cash flow forecasts and had not solicited interest rate quotes from their banking partners.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- Prepare a cash flow forecast so you know how much you can safely move into a higher-yield account.
- Solicit interest rate quotes from your banking partners periodically. Do not assume your current rate is still competitive.
- Check whether your investment policy already allows higher-yield accounts, and use them if it does.

> Across all of these audits, a consistent pattern appears. Problems rarely begin with fraud or misconduct. They begin with small gaps in oversight, unclear documentation, or policies that drift out of alignment with actual practice. Regular review of financial procedures, procurement policies, and board reporting practices helps prevent those small gaps from becoming larger governance failures.

## Respect in the Workplace

OWWL's Employee Assistance Program sent along an article this month on making respect the rule in the workplace.

In my experience, a good share of the disputes that end up needing mediation in a library come down to the same root cause, a lack of respect somewhere in the working relationships that hold a library together. Sometimes that is a lack of respect for the director's position and authority to manage day-to-day operations. Sometimes it is a misunderstanding of the board's role versus the director's. Sometimes it is a misunderstanding among staff about who is responsible for what, a staff member who is certain they know better than the director, or a patron who is certain they know better than staff. None of these start out as disputes about respect exactly, but that is often where they end up.

Here are a few examples of what this can look like in practice, drawn loosely from situations from the past year in libraries: 

- A trustee who routinely goes around the director to give direction straight to staff, undercutting the director's authority to manage the operation.
- A staff member who treats a disagreement over a schedule or an assignment as something to escalate straight to the board rather than working it out with a supervisor first.
- A board that treats the director's reports and recommendations as a formality rather than the professional judgment they represent.

None of this requires bad intentions to cause damage. Left alone, small breakdowns like these tend to grow into the kind of dispute that needs outside help to resolve.

If this sounds familiar, [Friday's Director Handbook Book Club session on the director-board relationship](#director-handbook-book-club-the-director-board-relationship) is a good place to look at where these roles and responsibilities are supposed to sit.

The EAP's own article, [Make Respect the Rule](https://www.theeap.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/TC-October-2026-Newsletter-Respect-Should-Be-the-Rule.pdf?mkt_tok=MjEyLVJCSS03MzAAAAGkaHEl0C-2HSX5ljgpXKJ3reeE-jEbPZJZ2WxITfYv8Jcfa32UUPsO8g97sIycZKuhXGZihtj1mbST4JIkGJu9POuT7p_JW-_hcNCq-urggUe2sSc), covers this topic more fully, including guidance on recognizing and responding to bullying.
