## E-Rate: Comment Submitted to the FCC

I have submitted OWWL Library System's comment to the Federal Communications Commission on the future of E-Rate. It speaks for all 42 member libraries, and it is built on what you told me.

### What the comment says

Thirty-five libraries sent input, and the comment relies on it throughout.

- **The cost.** Using each library's current discount, I estimated that without E-Rate our 42 libraries together would pay roughly $103,000 more every year for Internet service alone. Most would pay about $1,600 more, and a few would pay $4,000 to $7,100 more. That total does not include the Wi-Fi and network equipment that E-Rate now discounts.
- **Your stories.** The comment quotes many of you by library. It describes cuts to materials budgets, programs, and staff hours, equipment that could not be replaced, and tax increases that voters may not approve. This is the strongest part of the comment, thank you.
- **What we asked the FCC to do.** Continue the E-Rate program and keep its support for libraries. Keep the services libraries rely on, including Internet access, Wi-Fi, internal connections, and maintenance. Avoid changes, such as filtering or monitoring of patron devices, that add cost or burden for small libraries. Keep qualified E-Rate help available and affordable for libraries without technology staff.

## Appointed Trustees on Elected Boards Serve Only Until the Next Election

A Commissioner of Education decision issued on September 22, 2026 affects how trustee vacancies are filled at libraries whose trustees are elected by the voters. I know of one library with language in its charter that conflicts with the decision. If your library elects its trustees, it is worth checking whether yours does too. As long as your library has been following §226(4), which most are, then you won't have a problem. 

### The decision

In *Appeal of Squitieri* and *Appeal of Dahan* (Decision No. 18,853), the Commissioner held that Education Law §226(4) controls. The statute says that "where trustees are elected by the legal voters, the person so appointed to fill any such vacancy shall hold office until the next annual election of trustees."

A charter cannot change that. Section 226 lets trustees act "unless otherwise provided by law or by... charter," but the Commissioner held that the later, more specific proviso overrides that general language. The Assembly memo behind the statute says it is "inconsistent to have the voters of a district elect members to the board and still have the board retain the power to fill a vacancy for the balance of an unexpired term."

The Commissioner ordered the two libraries in those appeals to amend their charters within 60 days. One library's bylaw amendment was annulled, and the seats held by appointees go on the May 2027 ballot. The appointees stay on the board until then.

### What this means for your library

The statute and the decision apply to libraries whose trustees are elected by the voters. If that is your library, and your charter says the board fills a vacancy for the balance of the unexpired term, that process will need to be amended. A charter amendment goes through the Board of Regents.

The decision orders amendments only for the two libraries in the appeals, so it sets no deadline for anyone else. Until a charter is amended, the practical course is to follow the statute. An appointed trustee serves until the next annual election, and that seat goes on the ballot. Bylaws that already say an appointment does not create a new term, and that the seat is filled by election at the next annual election, are consistent with the decision.

This is not an urgent matter for most libraries, but it is something to correct the next time your library amends its charter. If you would like help with the amendment language or the board motion, reach out to me.

If you have a situation involving a mid-year appointment, let me know and I can help make sure everything is set up according to law.

## Minimum Standards Policy Webinar Recordings Are Now Captioned

The New York State Library has captioned the recordings from the webinar series it offered over the past several months and added them to a [new playlist on the New York State Library YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLP4FFY-z_o_w). Public library system staff and leadership presented the sessions.

The series followed the Board of Regents' approval of an amendment to paragraph (4) of subdivision (b) of section 90.2 of the Regulations of the Commissioner of Education. That paragraph covers Public and Association Library Minimum Standard #4, Board-approved written policies. The standard now names specific policies that must be reviewed and updated at least once every five years, or sooner if required by law.

- Selection of library materials and reconsideration of that selection
- Public usage of library space and meeting rooms
- Codes of conduct
- Confidentiality of library records
- Internal library policies on personnel, disaster preparedness, and financial control

The personnel policies should ensure consistent staff management and fair employment practices. The financial control policies should fulfill the board's legal and fiduciary responsibilities and promote fiscal oversight, accountability, and sustainable management.

The Public Library System Directors Organization (PULISDO) and the New York State Library have also updated the [Helpful Information for Meeting Minimum Public Library Standards](https://nyslibrary.libguides.com/publiclibrarystandards/policies) page to support libraries in developing or reconsidering these policies. Questions or suggestions for future programs can go to Tor Loney, Library Development Specialist at the New York State Library.

## This Week in OSC Audits

Below is a summary of this week's Office of the State Comptroller audits. They cover towns, cities, a village, a county, and two school districts across New York. The settings vary, but the themes are familiar ones for any public institution, including libraries. Fire district and fire company audits are excluded from this summary.


### Audit Takeaways

The five most important steps for libraries from this week's audits.

1. **Reconcile every bank account every month, with an independent review.** The person who reconciles should not be the person who handles deposits or can move money in the account.
2. **Get monthly financial reports to the board, and complete the annual audit.** File required annual financial reports on time, since late or missing filings were the common thread in several audits.
3. **Disable accounts the day someone leaves.** That includes email, Evergreen, and every other system, and it applies to vendors and consultants when their work ends. Compare active accounts against current staff on a regular schedule.
4. **Keep a current list of who holds keys, fobs, and alarm codes.** Collect keys and deactivate access on a person's last day, and review the list at least once a year.
5. **Document competition and board approval for purchases.** Keep the quotes on file, include professional services and insurance in your procurement policy, and have the board audit claims before they are paid.

### Financial Records and Bank Reconciliations

Three audits found that officials were not keeping accurate financial records or reconciling their accounts.

The audit of a city's Clerk-Chamberlain was the most severe. About $9.9 million in transactions were unrecorded or improperly accounted for, and bank accounts had not been reconciled since December 2022. Twenty-one of 22 bank accounts showed discrepancies, $5.3 million in needed cash transfers between accounts were never made, and $1.6 million in retirement expenditures went unrecorded. Recorded cash was $1.0 million (20%) lower than the actual $5.0 million bank balance.

In a town, the Supervisor's records overstated payroll-related liabilities by $236,089 and misclassified or improperly recorded $384,515 in revenues. No monthly financial reports went to the Board.

In a second city, reconciliations of 25 bank accounts (about $39 million) contained unsupported or inaccurate items, which overstated cash by $28,786. Outstanding checks more than seven years old ($13,918) stayed on the monthly reconciliations without verification, and deposits in transit were overstated by $39,361. The contracted comptroller had online access to the bank accounts while also preparing the reconciliations, with no independent review. Receivable control accounts were not reconciled to their detail records, leaving unreconciled differences of $177,006 for real property taxes and $11,197 for water and sewer.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- Reconcile every account, not just the main operating account, and do it monthly. A gap that goes unnoticed for years is far harder to untangle than one found in thirty days.
- The person who reconciles an account should not be the person who handles deposits or has the ability to move money in it. If your library relies on a bookkeeper or outside accountant, someone else on the board or staff should review the reconciliation.
- Old outstanding checks and unsupported deposits in transit deserve a look each month. They are easy to carry forward and easy to overlook.
- Boards should receive financial reports every month, and they should be reports the board can actually read and question.

### Annual Financial Reports and Transparency

A statewide audit of towns found that for fiscal year 2025, only 593 of the state's 933 towns (64%) filed their Annual Financial Reports on time. Another 181 (19%) filed late, and 159 (17%) did not file at all. Among the 18 towns examined from fiscal year 2024, most had not submitted their reports, and most boards had not conducted the required annual audit.

A separate audit of a town showed what that looks like in one place. The Board did not provide for a timely annual audit of the Town's financial records for fiscal year 2024, and the Comptroller did not prepare or file the 2024 Annual Financial Report. Cash receipts were not fully recorded, and Board members received no monthly budget reports or balance sheets. The Supervisor's audit above adds to the pattern. That town's 2023 report was filed 738 days late, and its 2024 and 2025 reports were still unfiled when the audit ended.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- The annual audit of the treasurer's records, and the filing of required annual financial reports, are legal obligations for the governments covered by these audits. They are also good practice for any board. The same principle applies to whatever annual financial filings your library makes.
- A missed filing is a warning sign by itself. If your library has ever fallen behind on a required report, catching up is worth prioritizing.
- Monthly reports to the board are what make an annual audit meaningful. Without them, the board learns about problems only after the year ends.

- [A town, Transparency of Fiscal Activities](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/statewide-audit/2026/09/25/town-rotterdam-transparency-fiscal-activities-s9-25-36)

### [A school district: Cybersecurity and Network Accounts](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/school-district/2026/09/18/lawrence-union-free-school-district-information-technology-2026m-56)

Cybersecurity is a standing area of concern, and this audit is worth reading closely. Auditors reviewed how the district managed network user accounts and delivered IT security awareness training. The district had 494 enabled nonstudent accounts on one domain and 175 on a second. Of those, 250 were unnecessary and should have been removed. That included 162 accounts belonging to former employees, vendors, and consultants, and 88 service accounts. The district had no written policy for managing accounts. Training was generally provided, but documentation of completion was missing for one of ten employees reviewed.

This connects directly to work the System has been doing with member libraries. The multifactor authentication rollout for Evergreen (covered above) and the required Email and Evergreen trainings both exist to reduce the chance that an unneeded or compromised account becomes a way in. An account belonging to someone who left months ago is exactly the kind of gap this audit describes.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- Disable a departing staff member's accounts, at every system they touch, when they leave. Do the same for vendors and consultants when their work ends.
- Write down who is responsible for creating, reviewing, and removing accounts. In this audit, the missing written policy was part of the problem.
- Compare your list of active accounts against your current staff on a regular schedule, and remove what is left over.
- Keep a record that required security training was completed, such as the certificate. A missing record counts as a gap even if the training happened.

### [A school district: Building Access](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/school-district/2026/09/25/greene-central-school-district-building-access-s9-26-3)

Auditors reviewed how the district managed its building access accounts and devices (key cards and fobs). The district had 350 active access accounts and 359 active devices. Eighteen employees held duplicate active key cards, and eight active non-employee key cards were unnecessary, including three that officials could not locate. The district had no written policy assigning roles for managing accounts and distributing key cards, and no periodic review of whether active accounts were still needed.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- Keep a current list of who holds a key, fob, or alarm code, and reconcile it when staff or volunteers change.
- Collect keys and deactivate access on a person's last day.
- Assign one person responsibility for the list, and review it at least once a year.

### Procurement and Oversight of Purchasing

Two audits looked at how local governments buy goods and services.

A village purchased more than $5 million in goods and services during the audit period, including a $3.2 million wastewater treatment plant project. Officials did not document competition for $1.3 million of those purchases. That included $281,444 in purchases without competitive bids, 13 professional services and insurance contracts totaling $992,544 with no documented competition, and 16 purchases totaling $100,176 without the required written quotes.

In a town, the audit of its ambulance service found 14 purchases worth $59,674 made without the competitive quotes required, and an ambulance bought for $173,000 through a group purchasing organization that may not have met the legal conditions for using one. The same audit found that the Board did not ensure proper billing and collection of $235,902 in ambulance charges, and that $17,320 in write-offs were not approved. One employee received $2,114 in pay increases that the Board had not authorized, and the Board did not effectively audit 17 claims totaling $13,552.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- Professional services and insurance are easy to leave out of a procurement policy, yet the village audit found nearly $1 million in them without documented competition. Check that your policy covers them.
- Keep the quotes and the comparison in the file. If it was not written down, auditors will treat it as if it did not happen.
- If your library buys through a purchasing group or state contract, confirm that the conditions for using it are met, and keep proof.
- Board approval matters for write-offs, pay rates, and claims. A board should audit claims before they are paid, not just sign the warrant.

Audits in this group:

- [A village, Procurement](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/village/2026/09/18/village-moravia-procurement-2026m-82)
- [A town, Ambulance Service Financial Operations](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/town/2026/09/25/town-macedon-ambulance-service-financial-operations-2026m-65)

### [A county: Court and Trust Funds](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/county/2026/09/18/broome-county-court-and-trust-2026-ct-2)

Auditors examined whether a county properly controlled and accounted for court and trust funds from January 2024 through May 2026. The County Treasurer had not established adequate procedures to make sure all court and trust funds were received, accounted for, and reported. Reconciliations between the Treasurer, the County Clerk, and the Surrogate's Court Clerk were not done on time. The Treasurer had already begun addressing abandoned property after correspondence with another county and the State Comptroller in July 2025. Auditors recommended annual reconciliations with both clerks and timely payment of abandoned property to the State Comptroller.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- If your library holds trust, endowment, or restricted gift funds, make sure your records agree with those of whoever else tracks them, such as a bank, a foundation, or a Friends group, and compare them at least once a year.
- Uncashed checks and unclaimed payments are worth reviewing each year to see whether state unclaimed property rules apply.

> Across all of these audits, a consistent pattern appears. Problems rarely begin with fraud or misconduct. They begin with small gaps in oversight, unclear documentation, or policies that drift out of alignment with actual practice. Regular review of financial procedures, procurement policies, and board reporting practices helps prevent those small gaps from becoming larger governance failures.
