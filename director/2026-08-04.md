## Policy Reminder: Submit Tech Support, Evergreen, and Cataloging Requests as Tickets

All tech support, Evergreen, and cataloging requests need to go through the ticketing system rather than a direct email to System staff, even when you know exactly who usually handles a given issue. This isn't a new rule. It's already outlined in the [System and Member Library Relationship Policy](https://owwl.org/system/policies/system-and-member-library-relationship-policy/) and the [Computer and Technology Support Policy](https://owwl.org/system/policies/computer-support-policy/).

Here's why it matters.

Tickets create a documented history that's never deleted, so staff can search past resolutions if a similar issue comes up again. Email doesn't give us that, and inboxes need to be cleared out periodically. Staffing assignments also shift over time as duties change and coverage rotates, and a ticket means someone else on the team can pick up your request right away if the usual point person is out sick or on vacation. Routing every request through the same process also keeps things fair across all 42 member libraries.

A direct email to System staff about tech support, Evergreen, or cataloging will now get an automated reply pointing you to a ticket. This isn't meant to slow anyone down. It's meant to make sure your request is tracked and routed to the right person as quickly as possible.

Requests can go to any of these addresses, depending on the issue:

- **Technology support:** `support`
- **Evergreen support:** `evergreen`
- **Cataloging support:** `cataloging`

When in doubt, send it to `support` and we'll move it to the right queue.

Multiple staff monitor the ticket queue every day, so a ticket is still the most reliable way to get your question to the right person quickly.

## Know Your Language Access Rights Brochures for Patrons from Piety

The Outreach Department (Piety) is coordinating a group order of Know Your Language Access Rights brochures. The brochures are available in 26 languages, including English, and are meant to inform patrons of their language access rights.

If your library wants to join the group order, respond to [this form](https://forms.gle/ZwknwpuPpv4aKCCF8) by Wednesday, August 19, 2026.

### Background on the Language Access Law

New York's [Language Access Law](https://ogs.ny.gov/new-york-state-language-access-law) took effect in July 2022. It requires NYS agencies that interact with the public to provide interpretation services and translate key documents into the state's most commonly spoken languages.

Libraries aren't required to comply with this law, but being aware of it helps you inform and assist patrons, and providing language access proactively helps build a welcoming environment for your community. It's also worth watching, since these requirements could expand to cover libraries in the future.

To see which languages other than English are most commonly spoken in your county, check the [interactive language dashboard](https://ogs.ny.gov/language-dashboard).

### Resources from the NYS Office of Language Access

The [Language Access Resource page](https://ogs.ny.gov/language-access-resources) has best practices for translation projects, working with an interpreter, and hosting virtual meetings, along with a glossary of inclusive language.

The [Community Engagement and Collaboration page](https://ogs.ny.gov/community-engagement-and-collaboration) outlines the ways libraries and other community organizations can partner with the Office of Language Access.

A recording of a recent webinar, [Learn about Language Access Rights & Resources in New York State](https://us02web.zoom.us/rec/share/QdFLS1yTJ_5fjE_8tXbmLplyYA9HpCRA1LlsxVDZ3EGCB-M00Imwn6BsTfxsOK4I.7axIFNTJpp4oLza8?pwd=DJszXmh5-hEv6kHdLAAAIAAAAHEjw_3jJEy2Kd5Dnaq28CjiJ-NglnP0xg30jgpMRRcf9wx4mubvk4zL_1_oe5Xx6TAwMDAwNA), is also available, along with the [presentation slides (PDF)](https://nyslibrary.libguides.com/ld.php?content_id=85665657).

## Disabling Accounts and Avoiding Shared Logins Isn't Optional

Two of this week's Office of the State Comptroller audits are worth pulling out of the general roundup below and looking at on their own, because they get at something OWWL takes seriously: who has access to your systems, and how quickly that access goes away when it should.

### [A school district — Building Access](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/school-district/2026/07/10/auburn-enlarged-city-school-district-building-access-s9-26-13)

Auditors reviewed the district's building access system, which had 1,276 active accounts. Twenty-six employees and one non-employee each held two active badges at once. Thirty-two percent of the non-employee accounts reviewed were not disabled when no longer needed, and among shared badges reviewed, 56 percent didn't comply with the district's own procedures and 85 percent weren't needed at all. The root cause was that officials had written account-management procedures but weren't following them, and weren't conducting periodic reviews.

### [A town — Information Technology](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/town/2026/07/17/town-amherst-information-technology-2025m-120)

A separate audit found 11 unneeded network service accounts still active, including two with administrative privileges. Three departing employees' network accounts remained active for more than 30 days after they left. A hardware inventory review found 30 of 272 devices missing from records. The IT Director wasn't aware of these gaps because periodic account access reviews weren't being performed.

### Why this isn't just an OWWL preference

New York's own state auditors publish the standard they hold local governments and school districts to, and it's specific. The Comptroller's Information Technology Governance guide states plainly that "former employees' accounts should be disabled on the day they leave local government or school employment," and that "every user should have and use their own network user accounts (usernames and passwords)," not shared ones, because activity on a shared account can't be traced back to a single person. The guide also expects a periodic comparison of active employees against active accounts, so anything left over gets caught and disabled. These aren't rules OWWL invented. They're the standard the state applies when it audits any local government entity, libraries included.

For libraries, two actual state laws raise the stakes further. CPLR §4509 makes library records identifying who requested or borrowed which materials confidential by law, disclosable only with consent or under a court order or subpoena. Our new [Confidentiality of Library Records Policy](https://docs.owwl.org/pub/Community/PolicyLibrary/Confidentiality%20of%20Library%20Records%20Policy.docx) (covered above) is built to meet that requirement. The SHIELD Act (General Business Law §899-bb) separately requires "reasonable" administrative and technical safeguards for the private information libraries hold on patrons and staff. An account that's still active after someone leaves, or a login shared among several staff members, is exactly the kind of gap that undermines both. If untraceable or unauthorized access leads to a patron's borrowing history getting out, that's not a hypothetical. It's the confidentiality law and the security law both failing because of one skipped step.

That's also the difference between a shared account and sharing an individual account, and why OWWL treats both as a problem, not just one of them:

- **A shared account** (a generic circ-desk login several people use) means nobody's individual activity can be reconstructed if something goes wrong, and disabling it when one person leaves affects everyone else still using it.
- **Sharing an individual account** ("just log in as me") is worse in a different way. It puts one person's name on activity they may not have actually performed, and it means that account, and its password, has to stay active and known to other people for as long as anyone still relies on it, long after it should have been disabled or changed.

This is why OWWL asks every library to disable a departing staff member's accounts, at every system they touch, the same day the person leaves, and why we push back when a library wants to keep using one shared login across multiple staff. It's not extra caution for its own sake. It's what New York's own audit standard expects, and what the confidentiality and security laws that already apply to your library require you to be able to demonstrate.

Questions on setting this up at your library? Reach out to me.

## This Week in OSC Audits

Below is a summary of the rest of this week's Office of the State Comptroller audits. The audits below examine two towns and a village across New York. The settings vary, but the themes are consistent ones for any public institution, including libraries. Fire district and fire company audits are excluded from this summary.

### [A town — Town Clerk/Tax Collector](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/town/2026/07/10/town-cato-town-clerktax-collector-2026m-34)

Auditors reviewed about $5.8 million in collections handled by the Town Clerk between January 2023 and February 2026. The former Clerk did not deposit or remit collections within the timeframes required by New York Town Law: roughly $54,000 in clerk fees and $2.8 million in real property tax collections were deposited late, and about $883,000 in real property tax collections were remitted late to the Town Supervisor. Records were generally accurate, but the delays increased the risk that collections could be lost, unaccounted for, or misappropriated.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- Recording collections accurately isn't the same as depositing and remitting them on time. Both matter, and late deposits create real exposure even when the books eventually balance.
- If your library handles cash collections (fines, fees, program revenue), confirm the interval between collection and deposit matches your own financial policy, not just what's convenient day to day.

### [A town — Employee Benefits](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/town/2026/07/17/town-amherst-employee-benefits-2025m-90)

Reviewing 116 employees, auditors found 114 instances of improper vacation carryover (13,058 hours, valued at $671,338), 12 instances of unauthorized leave awards (387 hours, $24,618), and 21 cases of unauthorized compensatory time accrual (923.6 hours, $45,669). Twenty-two stipend payments totaling $328,155 lacked documentation supporting the extra duties they were meant to compensate. Of 78 separation payments totaling $2.5 million, errors and policy inconsistencies produced potential overpayments of $334,852. Auditors traced the root cause to decentralized recordkeeping and department heads unfamiliar with the applicable collective bargaining agreements.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- Leave accrual and separation payments are two of the easiest places for small, repeated errors to add up to a large number. A periodic reconciliation against the actual contract or policy language, not institutional memory of it, is worth the time.
- Stipends and extra-duty pay need the same documentation trail as any other payroll expense. If nobody could produce the reason for a stipend on request, that's worth fixing before an outside reviewer asks the same question.

### [A village — Transparency of Fiscal Activities](https://www.osc.ny.gov/local-government/audits/village/2026/07/17/village-richburg-transparency-fiscal-activities-2025m-116)

The Board did not conduct or arrange the annual audit of the Clerk-Treasurer's records required for fiscal year 2023, and the Clerk-Treasurer did not file Annual Financial Reports for fiscal years 2019 through 2023. Accounting records were incomplete: $91,194 in cash receipts went unrecorded in 2023, bank reconciliations weren't prepared that year, and $1,873 in property tax collections sat undeposited between June 2022 and May 2024. The Clerk-Treasurer ultimately admitted taking $1,060 from the Village in fiscal year 2024, was arrested, and pleaded guilty to petit larceny in April 2026.

**Takeaways for libraries**

- The board's annual audit of the treasurer's or clerk-treasurer's records is a legal requirement, not a courtesy. Skipping it removes the one internal check that might catch a shortfall early.
- Years of unfiled Annual Financial Reports are themselves a red flag, independent of what an eventual audit finds. If your library's AFR filings have ever lapsed, treat catching up as a priority regardless of what else is going on.

> Across all of these audits, a consistent pattern appears. Problems rarely begin with fraud or misconduct. They begin with small gaps in oversight, unclear documentation, or policies that drift out of alignment with actual practice. Regular review of financial procedures, procurement policies, and board reporting practices helps prevent those small gaps from becoming larger governance failures.

## A Top 10 for Handling Contracts, MOUs, and Agreements

Every contract, MOU, or agreement your library signs starts as someone else's draft, written to protect their interests first. That's not a red flag on its own. It's just how templates get written. The problem comes when a library signs one as-is, without reading it, understanding what each clause is actually asking for, and pushing back where a term doesn't fit a public institution.

The same discipline applies whether you're negotiating an e-resource license, a service contract with a vendor, a memorandum of understanding with your sponsoring municipality, or a cooperation agreement with your Friends group. None of those is an enforceable contract in exactly the same way a signed vendor agreement is, but each still deserves the same care, since it's what keeps a shared relationship (payroll, insurance, building maintenance, fundraising, volunteer support) from drifting into confusion about who's actually responsible for what.

Here are ten things worth checking in any contract, before you sign it and again before you renew it.

1. **Read it, all of it, before you sign or renew.** A contract isn't final until both sides agree it is. Copy the agreement, read through it once for anything that gives you pause, then write down why each flagged item is a problem before making any edits.
2. **Watch for confidentiality clauses that cover pricing, not just proprietary information.** A vendor's platform architecture is fair to protect. The price you're paying usually isn't, and a public library generally can't promise pricing confidentiality it has no authority to deliver. Records of NY public libraries and systems are generally reachable under FOIL (Public Officers Law Article 6).
3. **Match auto-renewal terms to your budget cycle.** A renewal term that outlasts your library's appropriation cycle isn't something a public entity can agree to. A non-appropriation clause, allowing termination without penalty if funding isn't allocated, is often what makes a multi-year agreement signable in the first place.
4. **Push back on open-ended indemnification.** Many public libraries have no legal authority to indemnify a private party at all. If a contract asks your library to cover a vendor's losses or legal fees, that's not something to sign around.
5. **Look at what the warranty disclaimer actually disclaims.** Some disclaimers are reasonable. Others also waive any promise that the vendor holds the rights it's licensing to you, or any service-level commitment at all. Ask for a rights warranty and a real remedy if the service goes down.
6. **Require notice and a cure period before termination.** A clause that lets either party end the agreement immediately, with no notice and no chance to fix the problem, leaves your library with no room to respond if something goes wrong on your end, and no leverage if it goes wrong on theirs.
7. **Check governing law and venue.** A distant state, or one with vendor-favorable statutes, can make your remedies theoretical even when you're in the right. Many New York public entities are directed to keep governing law and venue in-state; if yours is, say so as a requirement, not a preference.
8. **Make sure the contract doesn't sign away rights the law already gives you.** Fair use, interlibrary loan, accessibility remediation, and preservation copying are protected under federal copyright law regardless of what a license says. A short clause stating that nothing in the agreement diminishes those statutory rights costs a vendor nothing and protects your library and your patrons.
9. **Don't let a vendor change signed terms through a posted webpage.** A clause treating continued use as acceptance of whatever terms are currently posted online works for consumer software. It doesn't belong in a negotiated agreement your board approved. Require that amendments be mutual, in writing, and signed by both parties.
10. **Protect patron data on its own terms.** Know what's collected, who else can access it, how long it's kept, and how fast you'll be notified of a breach. In New York, CPLR 4509 makes library records identifying who requested or used library materials confidential, and the SHIELD Act sets separate data security and breach-notification expectations. Our [Confidentiality of Library Records Policy](https://docs.owwl.org/pub/Community/PolicyLibrary/Confidentiality%20of%20Library%20Records%20Policy.docx) (covered above) is built around these same requirements, so any vendor handling patron data should be held to at least that standard. A vendor's own breach-notification timeline needs to sit inside these requirements, not replace them.

None of this is legal advice, and it isn't a substitute for having your own counsel review anything your library is about to sign. It's a starting checklist for knowing what to look for and which asks are reasonable to make. If you have questions about a contract, an MOU, or a specific clause, reach out to me and we'll work through it together.
