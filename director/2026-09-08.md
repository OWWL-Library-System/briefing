## Minimum Standards Compliance: Action Required

Last year, OWWL began confirming compliance with New York State Minimum Standards to satisfy the System's requirement to file assurances with the State. This year, the process is simpler.

- At your September or October board meeting, add a motion or resolution confirming your library is in compliance with Minimum Standards.
- After the meeting, send me the link to your board minutes once they are posted online. Post them within two weeks of the meeting, per Open Meetings Law. This satisfies the System's Assurance requirement.
- The deadline is October 31, 2026. As with last year, any hold-ups delay state aid for every library in the System, not just the one that is late.

This is not a new requirement. Libraries have been expected to do this for several years now.

**Resources**

- [Minimum Standards Compliance Policy](https://owwl.org/system/policies/minimum-standards-compliance-policy/)

Sample motion for your board:

> [TRUSTEE NAME] moves that the [Library Name] Board of Trustees affirms that the library is in full compliance with the New York State Minimum Standards for Public and Association Libraries as outlined in Commissioner's Regulation §90.2, and further directs [TRUSTEE NAME] to email the Executive Director of the OWWL Library System a link to the board meeting minutes once posted to the library's website, within two weeks of the meeting, in accordance with the New York State Open Meetings Law. [TRUSTEE NAME] seconds, and the motion passed unanimously.

## E-Rate Funding: A Concern Every Library Should Have on Its Radar

E-Rate funding is worth watching closely right now. OWWL files E-Rate on behalf of the System under two categories, Category 1, which covers about 90 percent of your library's internet bill, and Category 2, which pays for 80% of wifi access points, switches, firewalls, and the licensing that comes with them. Every library in the System draws on one or both, so a change to either category affects everyone, not just the libraries that use it most heavily.

The FCC opened a wide-ranging review of the E-Rate program this summer, a combined Notice of Proposed Rulemaking and Further Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (FCC 26-41), adopted June 25 and released June 26, 2026. 

**The most significant part of it is not a proposed rule but a question: whether E-Rate should continue in its current form at all.** 

The FCC notes that "virtually all schools report having broadband connectivity" today and asks whether the program's original purpose has already been met and whether it should be limited or sunset. A former FCC Commissioner has separately argued the agency may lack clear legal authority to fund schools and libraries the way the program currently operates. A coalition that includes the American Library Association and the Schools, Health & Libraries Broadband Coalition has filed in opposition, arguing Congress mandated the program by statute and the FCC cannot unilaterally narrow or end it.

Separately, the FCC is proposing new administrative rules that would affect OWWL directly as the entity that files Category 1 and Category 2 applications on behalf of member libraries, including a new annual consultant certification and disclosure form and individual registration requirements for consultants who currently register only at the firm level.

A public comment period is open. The firm deadline has not been set yet, since it runs 60 days from Federal Register publication, which had not occurred as of this review.

This is an informational note for now. Expect a fuller call to action once the comment deadline is set and there is a clearer picture of what each library may want to do.

## Conflicts of Interest: The Gray Zone

Most conflict-of-interest conversations start and end with the obvious cases. New York's Not-for-Profit Corporation Law (§715) requires a board to identify and manage related party transactions, cases where a trustee or officer stands to benefit financially from a decision the board makes. Nepotism in hiring or contracting falls in the same category. Most conflict-of-interest policies are built around exactly this. Disclosure, recusal from the vote, and documentation in the minutes. These are the conflicts boards are best at catching, because they are the ones the law and most existing policies already name.

### The Duty of Loyalty and the trustee's oath behind it

§715 is a floor, not the full picture. Public library trustees also take an oath of office under Public Officers Law §10 before serving, promising to faithfully and impartially discharge the duties of the position. (Association library trustees are not public officers, so the statutory oath does not apply the same way, though many boards ask incoming trustees to affirm the same commitment as a matter of practice). On top of that, every trustee carries a Duty of Loyalty to the library, a baseline governance obligation that does not wait for a specific transaction to trigger §715's disclosure requirements. The Duty of Loyalty means a trustee's decisions belong to the library, not to any other loyalty the trustee happens to be carrying into the room. A situation can fail that broader duty well before it ever fails the narrower legal test, and a board that only checks §715 is checking the wrong thing.

### The conflicts that do not show up on that list

A much larger set of situations that have been popping up in libraries create tension in a board's judgment without breaking the law specifically.

- A Friends-of-the-Library officer or active volunteer who also serves as a trustee. The Friends group's own priorities, fundraising, events, gift acceptance, are the kind of thing a trustee is also asked to weigh in on independently. In practice, Friends members often have a hard time setting aside their Friends-of-the-Library hat when they are at the table making decisions for the library.
- A liaison appointed to sit with the board on behalf of the Friends, a municipality, or another outside group. A liaison's role is to carry information between that group and the library, not to participate in the full meeting or influence votes. In practice, liaison seats often carry more informal sway over the board's decisions than the role was ever meant to have.
- A trustee's close personal friend or distant family member joining the same board. No money changes hands, but personal loyalty is hard to fully separate from institutional judgment on a difficult personnel matter or a contested vote. Patterns like this can drift into cronyism, with the board leaning on personal feelings rather than the library's best interest.
- Someone whose day job is working at a different library, an affiliate organization, or serving as a trustee elsewhere. Two peer libraries are not directly connected under the Not-for-Profit Corporation Law's related-party rules, so this pattern does not trigger that framework. A person's professional frame of reference is still shaped by the institution they run full time, though, and a board has no reliable way to know when that is shaping their input. The imbalance sharpens further within the same library system, or between partner organizations that already work closely together, where one library can end up with an outsized voice in a peer library's decisions that does not run the other way. The same holds for someone serving as an official in another capacity elsewhere. A trustee who keeps measuring this library's decisions against how a different organization handles governance can become a distraction, and blur what the board's own priorities actually are.
- A vendor, contractor, or landlord who does routine business with the library and also sits on its board. This one can shade into an actual related-party transaction under §715, not just a judgment call, and is worth watching closely for that reason.
- A local elected official whose other public role intersects with the library's funding or facilities. Education Law §260(4) only disqualifies a sitting member of the municipal council or board that appoints library trustees from serving as a trustee in that same municipality, and only for municipal public libraries with council-appointed boards. Plenty of other overlaps between a library and local government fall outside that narrow rule.

### Why are these worth look at even when nothing has gone wrong?

Conflict-of-interest policies exist to protect a board's decision-making process from divided loyalty, not only to catch money changing hands. A board does not need to prove misconduct to decide that a particular seat, or a particular pattern, does not serve its own effectiveness. Building eligibility or recusal norms around patterns like these is common, well-accepted nonprofit board practice, not because the pattern is illegal, but because a board is entitled to organize itself to protect clear, focused decision-making.

The discomfort itself is worth naming too. A trustee carrying one of these affiliations may genuinely believe it is not affecting their judgment, and they may be right. But the director and the other trustees are the ones who have to decide, every time a related topic comes up, whether to speak as candidly as they otherwise would in front of that person. That is a real cost to the board's effectiveness, whether or not the affiliated trustee ever does anything wrong. A pattern does not have to rise to an actual conflict of interest to still be bad for the library.

### What a board can do about it

A board that wants to get ahead of this does not need a legal finding first. A few places worth starting.

- Revisit your own conflict-of-interest policy's disclosure requirements. Many only ask about financial interests. Consider whether non-financial affiliations belong there too.
- Ask new trustee candidates to disclose affiliations like these at nomination rather than after the fact, including another library's staff role, a close personal relationship to a sitting trustee or staff member, or an active Friends role.
- Recusal from a specific vote or discussion is often enough. It does not require removing anyone from the board.
- Some boards adopt eligibility criteria in their bylaws that apply only to future candidates, if a particular pattern is a standing concern.
- Where it is an option, the cleanest path is often for a candidate to wait until an existing conflict has resolved on its own before joining the board, rather than joining and managing around it from day one.
- If a trustee recognizes that they are the one carrying the conflict, the most direct step is often to step back from the board, or from that specific seat, until it resolves, rather than waiting for the rest of the board to raise it first.

None of this requires assuming the worst about anyone currently serving. A pattern like these does not have to break §715 or any written policy to still be worth a second look. It is worth taking a look at your own conflict-of-interest policy and your board's current composition with this broader list in mind, and deciding for yourselves where the lines should sit.

The library comes first. A director's role is to carry out the mission, vision, and policies the board sets, and a trustee's role is to govern in a way that makes the best possible use of public dollars. Personal relationships, personal feelings, or personal beliefs have no place getting in the way of building the best possible library. The public owns the library, and it deserves an organization held to that standard.
