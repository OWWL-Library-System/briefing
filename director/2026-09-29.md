## E-Rate: Your Input Is Due Tomorrow

This impacts every library in our System, and it is critically important.

**Where things stand.** The FCC's notice (FCC 26-41) was published in the Federal Register on August 14. Public comments are due October 13, 2026.

The core issue is whether E-Rate, which covers roughly 90 percent of your library's internet bill (Category 1) and 85 percent of your wifi and network equipment costs (Category 2), should continue in its current form, be scaled back, or end.

## Final Reminder: Email and Evergreen Security Training Due September 30

Tomorrow, September 30, is the deadline for every holder of an OWWL email or Evergreen account to complete the two mandatory security trainings the OWWL Board of Trustees required in May. As of Monday, 17 accounts still had not completed them.

That is real progress. At the start of September, that number was closer to 80. Thank you to everyone who has made this a priority for their staff.

Please take a few minutes today to confirm that every staff member at your library, yourself included, has completed both courses.

- [Email Security](https://my.nicheacademy.com/owwl-staffacademy/course/80582) (approximately 15 minutes)
- [Systems Access and Confidentiality of Library Records](https://my.nicheacademy.com/owwl-staffacademy/course/92699) (approximately 25 minutes)

Staff log in at [my.nicheacademy.com/owwl-staffacademy](https://my.nicheacademy.com/owwl-staffacademy/) with their OWWL email address. The simplest way to check is to confirm you have a completion certificate on file for each staff member for both courses.

### What happens after September 30

Accounts that have not completed both trainings by September 30 will have their OWWL email and Evergreen passwords reset. This is a security measure, not a disciplinary action. Access stays suspended until the trainings are completed and confirmed. There will be no deadline extensions.

### Getting an account reactivated

Under the [Systems Access and Confidentiality of Library Records Policy](https://owwl.org/system/policies/systems-access-and-confidentiality-of-library-records-policy/), access is not restored without a written request from the library director to me. The request needs to include the following.

- A justification for why the required training was not completed by the deadline.
- Documentation confirming the account holder has signed your library's Systems Access and Confidentiality of Library Records Acknowledgment Form.
- Confirmation that all current authorized users at your library have been reviewed for compliance.

Send reactivation requests to me directly. Each one is added to my general work queue, so restoring an account may take time, up to the ten business days the policy allows. Until the account is restored, that staff member will not be able to use Evergreen or Zimbra.

Please do not fill that gap by sharing another staff member's login. A shared account is its own policy violation, and the library will be billed for it under the [Cost Recovery Schedule](https://owwl.org/system/policies/system-and-member-library-cooperation-policy/cost-recovery-schedule/). This is critical. The surest way to avoid that position is to confirm today that everyone on your staff has finished both trainings.

## Stronger Together: The System and Member Library Cooperation Policy

On September 9, the OWWL Board of Trustees adopted the [System and Member Library Cooperation Policy](https://owwl.org/system/policies/system-and-member-library-cooperation-policy/), which replaces the System and Member Library Relationship Policy first adopted in 2021. I want to come back to it this week, less for the specific provisions and more for the idea behind it.

OWWL is a cooperative. Each of our 42 member libraries keeps its own board, its own identity, and its own local decisions. In return, every library shares in a library system, digital collections, delivery, secure patron data infrastructure, technology support, continuing education, and purchasing power that no single library could sustain on its own.

That arrangement runs in both directions. The System commits to supporting member libraries with expertise, infrastructure, and advocacy. Member libraries commit to the governance and security practices that keep that shared infrastructure, and the trust our communities place in it, intact.

### What one library does reaches all of us

Because so much of what we do is shared, our libraries are more connected than they sometimes appear. A decision made at one library rarely stays local, for better or worse.

- **Security.** Evergreen and email are shared by all 42 libraries. A reused password, a phishing click, or an account left active after someone leaves does not only expose one library. It exposes everyone's patrons. That is why the [security trainings due tomorrow](#final-reminder-email-and-evergreen-security-training-due-september-30) and the [MFA rollout](#evergreen-multifactor-authentication-is-now-active) matter so much. Every library that takes these steps protects every other library too.
- **Policy decisions.** Patrons move between our libraries every day. How one library handles circulation, patron records, or confidentiality shapes what a patron expects at the next library down the road. Shared policies keep that experience consistent.
- **Reporting.** Some of the System's obligations to the State depend on every library. The annual Minimum Standards assurance is the clearest example. The System can only certify for all of us once each board has passed its compliance motion and sent it in. One missing motion is not just one library's paperwork.

The good news is that the same connection works in our favor. The [E-Rate comment](#e-rate-your-input-is-due-tomorrow) we are building right now will carry the weight of 42 libraries, not one. The shared catalog puts millions of items in front of patrons at even our smallest libraries. When one library solves a problem, every other library can benefit from what it learned.

### What the policy spells out

A few pieces of the policy worth knowing.

- **Independent governance.** Each library is governed by its own board. The System does not manage local operations, and its advice is advisory.
- **Asking for help.** Libraries are encouraged to contact the System on governance, policy, finance, outreach, continuing education, and day-to-day operations. Technology issues go through a support ticket so nothing gets lost. If you need to cancel or reschedule an on-site visit, please give at least 24 hours of notice so that time can go to another library.
- **Annual review.** The policy expects member library boards to review it as part of their regular annual review of System agreements. It does not require any separate documentation or reporting to the System.
- **Cost recovery.** When a library's non-compliance with a System policy causes the System to spend staff time or incur legal or corrective costs, those costs are recovered from that library under the [Cost Recovery Schedule](https://owwl.org/system/policies/system-and-member-library-cooperation-policy/cost-recovery-schedule/), rather than being absorbed by the other 41 libraries. The goal is fairness to every library that is doing its part, not penalizing anyone.

### Thank you

I want to say how much I appreciate all of you. Directors across this System answer the E-Rate call, guide their staff through required trainings, pilot new security tools (thank you again, Arcade and Newark), and look out for each other in ways most patrons will never see. That work is what makes OWWL a strong System rather than 42 libraries that happen to share a catalog.

Thank you for everything you do to make the whole System stronger.

## Patron Documents: Why Staff Should Not Edit or Receive Them

Printing, scanning, and copying are some of the most common things patrons ask staff to help with. Most of the time the request is simple, but questions have been raised about some of the challenges around this practice. 

A patron emails a document to a staff member's library email address, and the staff member opens it, edits or "fixes" it, and prints it for them. Sometimes the patron asks for this. Sometimes staff offer, or even insist, because it seems faster. It comes from a good place, but it puts staff in the middle of documents they should probably never be seeing, and it creates risk for the staff member and the library.

The concern here is not mainly that patrons are knowingly committing fraud (which is another issue that libraries can find themselves in). It is that staff are taking on patrons' documents and information at all.

### The risks when a patron's document passes through staff

- **Private information lands in the library's systems.** The documents patrons print are often the most sensitive ones they have, such as tax forms, benefit applications, bank statements, medical paperwork, court filings, and copies of IDs. Once one arrives in a staff inbox, the library is holding it. New York's SHIELD Act expects any organization holding electronic private information about New York residents (Social Security numbers, driver's license numbers, account numbers, and similar identifiers) to maintain reasonable safeguards for it and to notify affected people if it is exposed.
- **It stays there.** A document emailed to staff does not disappear when the patron walks out. It sits in the inbox, the sent folder, and often the Downloads folder or desktop of a staff computer. If that email account is ever compromised, and we have seen recently how that happens, every patron document sitting in it is exposed along with it.
- **Editing puts staff in the author's seat.** When staff change the content of a document, staff made the change, not the patron. If the document is later questioned, or if the patron says the library got it wrong, the library is now part of that story. Filling in or correcting the content of a form on someone's behalf can also drift into giving advice the library is not in a position to give. This is a significant concern of liability. 
- **Some edits are crimes.** Changing a document with the intent to defraud, deceive, or injure someone is forgery under New York Penal Law §170.00. A staff member who only suspects something is wrong is not part of a crime, because forgery requires knowing about the intent. However, a prosecutor can still try to prove that a staff member knew, and for some documents simply altering them is a crime. The safest course is for staff not to be the ones making the changes (WNYLRC Ask the Lawyer, "Assisting Patrons with Altering Legal Documents," August 10, 2017).

### A better default: straight to the print station

The simplest protection is to keep most print jobs from passing through staff at all. A patron's document should go from the patron to the print station, whether they print from a public computer or through your library's printing option if you offer one. Staff should not need to receive, open, or edit the file for that to work.

When a patron does ask for help, staff can still help a great deal without taking on the document.

- **Show, do not do.** Walk the patron through the steps while the patron keeps their own hands on the keyboard.
- **Do not accept patron documents into a staff email account or onto a staff computer.** Point the patron to the public computer or print station instead.
- **Do not edit content or take down information.** Staff should not type in, correct, or write down a patron's personal information, account numbers, or answers on a form.
- **Do not keep copies.** Misprints and abandoned print jobs should be shredded, not left in a recycling bin or a pile behind the desk.
- **Decline changes to official or signed documents.** If a patron asks staff to change what an official or signed document says, staff should decline, and a written policy gives them the basis to do so.

Showing a patron how to use the scanner or print a file is a service. Editing the content of a patron's document on their behalf is something else, and it puts the staff member and the library in a difficult spot.

### Why a written policy helps

The Ask the Lawyer opinion found no graceful way for staff to quietly stop helping without telling the patron why. Once staff assistance is routinely offered, withholding it without a stated basis raises due process concerns for public libraries and professional ethics concerns for association libraries. A written policy gives staff that basis, so the decision does not rest on one person's judgment in the moment.

Libraries that want to address this may find these pieces useful.

- **Staff expectations.** Job descriptions, the employee handbook, or volunteer letters can say that staff serve patrons "in accordance with established policies and procedures." A short desk procedure can then spell out that staff do not accept patron documents by email or edit the content of a patron's document.
- **A posted policy on illegal use of library equipment.** The opinion offers sample language that fits into an existing internet, technology, or patron conduct policy.

> Use of library equipment for illegal activity is forbidden. Examples of illegal activity include but are not limited to: manipulating illegal content, engaging in forgery (falsely altering documents), gaining unauthorized access to other computers or networks, and 3D printing of illegal devices. Staff assisting you, who suspect illegal activity, are authorized to discontinue assistance, and the library may discontinue your library access and contact law enforcement. Patrons using technology to alter official or signed documents should be aware that such activity may be perceived as potentially in violation of this policy.

- **A simple procedure with an appeal.** A staff member who is concerned steps back and checks with a supervisor. If access to a service is removed, the patron gets written notice and a way to appeal. Anything involving possible imminent harm, such as someone altering a Power of Attorney, goes straight to the person in charge rather than being decided alone at the desk.

Patron privacy does not change here. The records created by a patron's use of library computers and printers remain confidential under CPLR §4509, and staff are not expected to monitor what patrons are working on. Keeping patron documents out of staff hands is one of the best ways to protect that privacy.

If your library would like help working this language into an existing policy or desk procedure, reach out and we can look at it together.

**Sources**

- WNYLRC Ask the Lawyer, ["Assisting Patrons with Altering Legal Documents"](https://wnylrc.org/raq/assisting-patrons-altering-legal-documents) (August 10, 2017)
