## Why This Week's Security Issues Affect Every Library

This week's briefing covers three separate security issues. A data breach at a member library, a formal warning for shared user accounts, and a system-wide review that turned up a significant number of weak, outdated passwords. Each is serious on its own. Together, they highlight the fact that account security is not being taken seriously across the System, and that affects every library, not just the ones described below.

This is not something directors can set aside. A compromised account, a shared login, or a password pulled straight from a public breach list can each become the incident that costs your library money and puts patron and staff data at risk. None of this is distant or hypothetical. All three happened, or were found, this week, and all three need to be addressed now.

Please take this seriously. Communicate to staff that their actions are costing significant resources. Because of this, we are working to revise how we handle these issues and who pays for the damages incurred.

## A Data Breach and What It Means for Account Security

One of our member library directors' email accounts was recently compromised through unauthorized access. It went unreported for several weeks while messages were forwarded to an outside account. We've secured the account and are still assessing the damage. This is a significant breach, and we're in the early stages of the investigation.

Our attorney is involved, and this may need to be reported to the New York State Attorney General under the SHIELD Act and other cybersecurity laws. We're manually reviewing hundreds of messages to work out what personally identifiable information was exposed. I have a call with our attorney this week to coordinate that notice and determine who will be responsible for making the notice. 

Our last data breach cost the System several thousand dollars. This one is on track to cost about the same. This is not an abstract risk. It's a real cost, and it falls on all of us as a System.

I know this will most likely happen again to one of your accounts. That's not a reason to be fatalistic about it. It's the reason the steps below matter.

**Do this now.**

- Don't click links or open attachments in anything that feels even slightly off, especially messages about password resets, invoices, or "your account needs attention."
- If you notice anything unusual (a message you didn't send, a login notification you don't recognize, email going missing), investigate it right away. Don't wait to see if it resolves itself. You don't need to report every spam message, but oddities are worth flagging.
- If you use the same password for your OWWL email, Evergreen, or anything else, including personal accounts, change it now. Reusing a password isn't just a bad habit; it violates policy and can carry penalties.

**Password requirements, as a reminder.**

Under the Systems Access and Confidentiality of Library Records Policy, every password used to access OWWL systems (email, Evergreen, the reporting tool, LibCal, all of it) has to be:

- at least 12 characters long, 
- randomly generated rather than a recognizable word or phrase, 
- unique and never reused from a personal account or an old password, and 
- complex enough to hold up on its own without relying on a predictable substitution like adding "!" at the end.

A diceware passphrase, five or more randomly generated dictionary words strung together, meets this requirement and tends to be easier to remember than a string of random characters. Either approach works, as long as it's something you haven't used anywhere else. On a personal note, I use a password manager for all of my own passwords; Bitwarden has worked well for me (not an endorsement, just my own preference), and there are plenty of other options worth a look.

**A few general tips.**

Check the actual sender address, not just the display name, before trusting an email; attackers spoof display names constantly. Hover over a link before clicking it to see where it actually goes. Be suspicious of urgency; "act now or lose access" is a manipulation tactic almost every time, not an actual deadline. If an email asks you to log in, go to the site directly yourself instead of clicking through. When in doubt, ask. A quick message to `support` costs nothing. A compromised account costs weeks.

**General email management tips.**

- Unsubscribe from non-essential lists. Every newsletter or vendor list you skim at best is inbox noise that makes it easier for something that actually matters, like a bounce notice or a security alert, to blend in and get ignored. 
- Periodically audit your own mail rules and forwarding settings; most people set up a filter once and never look at it again, and a five-minute check every so often catches exactly the kind of thing that ran undetected for weeks in this incident. 
- Treat undeliverable or bounce notices as something to look at, not ignore; a sudden pattern of them could be a signal that something is wrong. 
- Triage instead of letting messages pile up, since a backlog of hundreds of unread messages trains you to skim subject lines and mass-ignore. 
- Separate "needs action" from "just FYI" with labels or folders you actually check. When in doubt about a subscription, unsubscribe rather than archive, since archiving just means it comes back next time. 
- Delete messages containing patron information, staff information, or other personally identifiable information as soon as they're no longer needed. Those are the messages that trigger notification to the attorney general. 

I know email isn't always the first thing on your radar during a shift. But because of how connected our IT infrastructure is, we are only as strong as our weakest password. Please take a few minutes to review your own account against the points above. Reach out if you have questions or want a hand strengthening your account security.

## Weak Passwords Found in a System-Wide Review

As part of ongoing account security work, we ran a review of password strength across staff and circulation accounts System-wide. It turned up a significant number of accounts using passwords that are old, weak, or that show up directly in public breach databases. 

Without naming any library, staff member, or the passwords themselves, here's a sense of what that review found, based on how long ago the password was last changed and how many times a matching password has appeared in known data breaches.

| Staff/Circ | Last Changed | Times Seen in Data Breaches |
| ---------- | ------------ | --------------------------- |
| Staff      | 2007-06-25   | 662,463                     |
| Staff      | 2007-06-25   | 388,490                     |
| Staff      | 2018-03-21   | 15,526                      |
| Staff      | 2007-06-26   | 16,878                      |
| Staff      | 2009-10-20   | 14,366                      |
| Circ       | 2013-09-10   | 360,570                     |
| Staff      | 2018-12-08   | 7,848                       |
| Staff      | 2017-11-21   | 4,269                       |
| Staff      | 2014-12-18   | 1,962                       |
| Staff      | 2021-05-24   | 1,049                       |
| Circ       | 2019-03-05   | 602                         |
| Staff      | 2020-04-02   | 460                         |

These are not hypothetical. This is a small sample of what we found. Twelve real OWWL accounts, staff email and circ accounts alike, that we were able to access with little effort because of the passwords in place. "Last Changed" is the last time the account's password was updated. "Times Seen in Data Breaches" is how many times that password has turned up in known data breach and are publicly available for anyone to search. Attackers running brute-force attacks work from those same lists, starting with the passwords that show up most.

None of the passwords found met the 12-character minimum required under the Systems Access and Confidentiality of Library Records Policy. Some hadn't been changed since 2007. This is a direct policy violation, and in several cases it's the kind of password that could be guessed or cracked in under a second. The most disappointing are the circ accounts that have passwords with with the library's name in them.  

**We're resetting the affected accounts this week.** If you or a member of your staff receives a password reset notice, it means the account was flagged as a significant security risk. Please treat it that way, and take the opportunity to set a new password that actually meets policy: at least 12 characters, randomly generated or a diceware passphrase, and not reused from anywhere else.

This review is not a one-time sweep. It's the start of an ongoing look at account security across the System. I'd rather we find and fix these accounts ourselves than have someone else find them for us.

Because these accounts are in violation of policy, the list will be brought to the OWWL Library System Board of Trustees for discussion at our next meeting to determine a course of action for these violations.

## Formal Warning Issued for Shared User Accounts

A formal warning has been issued to a library for sharing staff login credentials. If it is not resolved by the issued deadline, the library will be subject to a loss of Evergreen subsidy and other penalties. Luckily, the director in questions has been helpful and forthcoming about the situation and is willing to work to correct the issue.   

Sharing logins violates the Systems Access and Confidentiality of Library Records Policy. It also erases accountability and security. If several people are logging in under one account, there's no way to know who actually took a given action on that account, and that becomes a real problem the moment something needs to be investigated, as this week has shown.

If your library is doing this, whether at the circ desk, on a shared workstation, or anywhere else, stop now and ensure that all staff are using their own user account. It's a policy requirement, and it's the only way to keep everyone's actions properly separate and traceable.

## Congratulations to Sodus Community Library

On a happier note, the Sodus Community Library has been named a 2026 RRLC Library of the Year, one of only three libraries across the region to receive the honor this year.

The nomination pointed to what makes Sodus stand out: strong customer service, a wide range of programs from arts and crafts to mahjong, chess, and video gaming, author visits and local history presentations, a book club and story times, and collaborative programming with community partners on GED classes, tax assistance, digital literacy, and nutrition and exercise. Sodus also runs a seed library and offers home delivery for patrons who can't get to the building. On top of the programming, the nomination specifically called out an engaged staff, a Board committed to the freedom to read, and a Friends group that shows up for the library in every way it can.

RRLC also recognized Amanda Buell, Sodus's Program Coordinator, as a 2026 Library All Star, for the kind of patron-focused work that, as her nomination put it, "leaves people better than how she found them."

Congratulations to the whole Sodus team. This is well-earned recognition. Winners will be honored at the RRLC Member Event this fall, with more details to come.

Congratulations as well to this year's other Library of the Year winners, Hobart & William Smith Colleges' Warren Hunting Smith Library and Kendall Central School District Library, and to all of this year's Library All Stars.
